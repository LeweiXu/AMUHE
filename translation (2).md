# Chapter 332: The Dawn Crescent — One

There was not a single day I didn't vomit.

Roused at the crack of dawn with the sunrise, full armor on.

Forced to run with sandbags strapped to our backs.

Having been there from the very beginning, I still had it relatively easy. Most newcomers threw up right at that stage.

Next came an all-out twenty-ken sprint. The fastest runner each round was allowed to drop out and rest, but everyone else naturally had to keep going, repeating the sprints until they'd each taken first place. The more people there were, the more rounds that meant, and the later breakfast was pushed back — but the rule was absolute: no one ate until every last person had won a round.

If someone couldn't run anymore, someone else had to carry them to finish, or breakfast would never come.

After the meal, endless stretching.

Fortunately, a little while after I'd joined, the post-meal running — and the vomiting that came with it — was abolished. Originally there had been a post-meal jog meant to settle the stomach, but the young lady of the Christand household — the unit's founder — had witnessed the men throwing up afterward and delivered a sermon:

*'Honestly, what do you all think food is? From the fieldwork to the cooking, you should think more about the feelings of the people who made this meal.'*

And with that gracious lecture from Krische Christand, the practice was abolished.

Did she really think a single person in this world *enjoyed* vomiting after a meal?

There wasn't a man present who didn't feel a flash of murderous intent.

The stretching was its own kind of eternity. It didn't matter if you were young — even the older men weren't spared. Legs spread until your hips felt ready to split, your whole body stretched without mercy.

Exhausted from the morning, a break for meals, then stretching. By the time it was over, fatigue and loosened muscles left your arms and legs without strength, and only then did we move on to sword drills.

Downward cuts, sweeps, thrusts, upward slashes — varied combinations, fifty repetitions per set. Around this point, even the veterans started going hazy in the head.

Supposedly to prevent injuries when swords slipped from exhausted hands, the blades were wound round and round with cloth, making them unbearably heavy. At the peak of exhaustion, men passed out every single day.

It was right around then that the girl would show up for an inspection.

While we stood there barely breathing, swinging our swords like the walking dead, she watched without a shred of pity, munching cookies with an easy expression.

*'Hmm, they're slower to learn than Krische expected. Dagra, shouldn't we add more weight to the swords and their limbs?'*

And then she'd say something like that, on top of everything.

There's a point where anger loops back around to nothing. That was it.

*'Y-yes, ma'am. However……if we push them further, there is a risk of men injuring themselves……'*

*'Mmm……Krische wants to move on to the next stage soon, though. Watching them, there are still too many who rely on muscle. Krische thinks it would be better to push them just a bit further, right to the edge of being unable to move. As long as you give them proper meals and rest, they probably won't die.'*

*'Yes, ma'am. Understood……'*

*'Also, don't let anyone desert. If deserters came out of Krische's own unit at a time when we're supposed to be gathering soldiers, Krische would be too embarrassed to show her face to the head of the house.'*

Stifling a yawn, she said only what she had to say, then left.

Who was it that first called her the "mad rabbit"?

The polar opposite of her lovely appearance. Treatment without a shred of feeling, those purple eyes looking at us like we were insects.

There couldn't have been a soul who held any fondness for her at the time.

A great noble. The worst kind of spoiled young lady, issuing orders through her parents' authority.

That was what the girl called Krische Christand was.

The unit members were all commoners. And commoners already had an ingrained distrust of the nobility. The name Christand was known for its integrity, but her behavior was precisely the sort of thing every bad rumor about the aristocracy warned of — an arrogant creature who didn't consider commoners human. Everyone thought so.

We'd heard she was an adopted daughter, and if anything, that only made the outrage worse.

Just because she'd been lucky enough to be picked up by a general's household — how could she treat her own fellow commoners like this?

There was no question that every last one of us nursed both hatred and murderous thoughts toward her.

Conversely, Dagra — the one who directly issued the grueling orders — earned a measure of sympathy. We half-pitied him for being unable to defy that girl, and even felt he was one of us.

Dagra was a harsh training instructor, but you could see him trying to shield us from the girl's orders, which bordered on lethal, and many of us knew he was running himself ragged day and night to make sure we got proper rest.

Knowing that, you couldn't bring yourself to resent Dagra.

Thoughtful older hands like Kirik apparently suspected at the time that her appalling behavior might even be deliberate — that by making us focus our hatred on her, she was keeping us from directing hostility at Dagra, our direct instructor.

But that was reading too far into it, overthinking it.

Having come to know her since, what we saw and felt was almost entirely the truth.

Krische-sama could, fundamentally, be called a good person — but at the same time, she was a girl who was cold to the very core.

Back then, she literally could not have cared less about us, and if one or two of us had died during training, it surely wouldn't have bothered her much.

It was certainly a merciless enough training regimen for that.

At the time, the situation was what it was — the sudden civil war between the princess and the prince. There was no time; it was a crash course of do-or-die training.

Much of it was haphazard, the training dreamt up on a whim by that little princess. For those of us who went through the crash course in the "Black Century," the fellows trained later in the "Black Flag Special Service" had it remarkably lucky.

In truth, we had no idea what we were being put through, or why. Just run, just swing your sword, carry this barrel — nothing but hellish training.

The one mercy was that we were worked so hard our brains stopped functioning, leaving no room even for questions.

Despite being ravenous, we ate like dead men, and every night as we collapsed onto our beds we plotted ways to escape — only to fall asleep before we could act, defeated by sore muscles and exhaustion.

"……?"

By my reckoning, it was about two weeks in when I first noticed it.

In the evenings before dinner, there was a time slot where groups of five were made to haul insanely heavy barrels. Each squad had to carry at least two of the things — barrels so heavy a single person couldn't possibly manage one. In our squad, the two who lost at rock-paper-scissors shouldered the barrels while the other three supported them from behind, marching endlessly around Fort Belgash.

Unlucky enough to lose that day, I'd been stuck with the barrel, dreading the whole thing — and what struck me was its lightness.

The kind of thing you couldn't carry solo for half a ri, let alone ten ken — my back and the soles of my feet felt its weight as usual, but the sensation of being crushed beneath it was gone.

I wondered if I'd gotten lucky with a lighter barrel, but the men who'd brought them showed no sign of anything unusual.

Tilting my head but playing along, I groaned and strained like the other four, pretending it was heavy as I carried the barrel.

We were exhausted to begin with. Just walking was enough to feel like dying, and if anyone noticed the barrel was light, we'd probably be made to do it all over again.

I faked my way through, and by the time I lay down to sleep, the question had slipped from my mind and I was out cold — but the next day, it got stranger.

Swinging the heavy cloth-wrapped sword, I felt an odd lightness. My body was as exhausted as ever, but the sword was as light as swinging a tree branch, not even a wooden practice sword.

Yet my palm still felt its solid heft, and if I swung with too much momentum, the centrifugal force nearly pulled me off balance.

I thought maybe I'd simply gotten better with a sword, but I'd been a bouncer at a gambling den before this. I'd swung swords plenty and was no amateur. It was hard to believe I'd suddenly made some dramatic improvement at this stage.

"Something wrong, Koza? Tired?"

"No, sir! It's nothing, Instructor Dagra……sir!"

I answered in a hurry and kept swinging as usual, tilting my head.

I'd been pretending to struggle, but even after the day's drills I felt energy to spare. Two days in a row I lost at rock-paper-scissors and was stuck with the barrel, yet again I felt that strange lightness — and this time I even had the composure to look around.

Carrying these murderous barrels, you normally couldn't afford to look around. Eyes down, one foot in front of the other — that was normal.

But here and there, I spotted others who, despite panting hard, were glancing around as well — in other words, people who were clearly acting as if they were carrying something heavy.

I didn't think Dagra had lightened the barrels.

It was around then that my vague suspicion crystallized into a clear question: *something* was going on.

I knew there were people in the world who could only be called superhuman. The world's a big place — even after being assigned to this training unit, I'd seen several people whose physical abilities defied explanation. People who could effortlessly sprint twenty ken in the time it took me to run ten at full speed.

Back in the sticks, I'd once seen a slender, good-looking man toss a grown man with one hand — and this training unit was full of people like that.

The most conspicuous was probably Mia. Despite her cute face, she had monstrous strength, and in the sprints she was in the top group of superhumans at the time (though she later plummeted to near last place). Her build made it absurd that she could even keep up with hellish training that had grown men giving up.

She whined about the barrel carries being heavy and painful, but once she shouldered one, she walked along without batting an eye.

"What do you want, Koza? Mia and I are both sick of getting hit on……want your crotch kicked in like Bug?"

"Don't glare like that. I'm not as stupid as that guy. I just had something to ask Mia — or you'll do, too. I'm not here to interrupt your meal. Once I've got my answer, I'll leave."

Mia was basically always stuck to Kalua's side. Superhuman strength and speed aside, she was a country girl from a backwater village. Surrounded by nothing but rough men, Kalua was probably her only lifeline.

Same at meals — they sat apart from everyone else to eat. Kalua, hardened by a rough life, didn't hesitate to use force on anyone who got too close, and ever since the roughneck ringleader Bug had gotten thrashed, almost nobody went near them. Being too exhausted to bother was part of it, but even apart from that, one cold glare from Kalua was enough to put a chill down the spine of even a former bouncer like me. You could tell from those eyes that she was a woman who'd walked through plenty of bloodshed.

"You want to talk to me……?"

"Yeah. The speed, well, that's one thing, but……you're abnormally strong. I was curious if there was something behind it."

"S-strong……"

"It's just a fact. You don't look like you've got the power, and you don't look like you could carry those barrels. Those are the kind of barrels that make a musclebound hulk like Dors carry them with veins popping out of his forehead. ……I figured there must be some reason beyond just muscle, and that this godforsaken training is meant to teach us how to use that something."

It was Kalua who let out an impressed "Oh?" Mia's eyes were darting around, looking uncertain.

"From the look on your face, I'm not far off……as I thought, you know something. And the reason the instructor's always talking to you isn't just because he gives women special treatment, is it?"

"The way you're talking, it sounds like you're already sure. You're already aware of this 'something beyond muscle'?"

"W-wait, Kalua……!"

"Mia, your face gives everything away — you can't bluff. They told us to keep it a secret, more or less, but someone who's figured it out on their own and come to ask in person, you can probably tell them, right? There's been more and more of us, after all."

"I-I guess, maybe, but……"

Mia made a troubled face and Kalua looked exasperated.

*Well?* — her eyes asked, and I answered.

"I first noticed the strange lightness yesterday. Today during training I realized something was clearly off, and I came to ask you two since you seemed to know. Am I right?"

"Pretty much. This training is meant to teach us how to use that power……it's called virtual musculature through mana manipulation, physical augmentation."

"……Physical augmentation. And mana, you mean the stuff in magic tools? Like the eternal magic lamps?"

"Right. Everyone in this unit was selected for having the aptitude to manipulate it. That's why a runt like Mia is here too……looks aside, she could already use it from the start."

I looked at Mia, and her eyes darted away.

"Apparently it's usually inherited through bloodlines, so it's mostly nobles. But sometimes people are born with it out of nowhere, and that bunny-girl's been screening for them."

"……I see. That explains it."

"If you rely on muscle, apparently you can't use it well. This idiotic training is simply to exhaust the body completely……I was lucky I got the hang of it quickly. I'd always kind of had the sensation, even unconsciously."

Kalua was in the superhuman group — which meant they must all have been people who could already use it.

"Also, kind of a gross topic, but haven't you been going less — number one and number two — compared to other people? Before you can use mana to a certain degree there's a lot of individual variation, apparently, but in my case, I pretty much stopped as a kid."

"That's from the mana too?"

"Seems like it. Things the body doesn't need get converted into mana, apparently……and that mana keeps the body in order without you even noticing."

Now that she mentioned it, I recalled. I'd always been on the low side, but since coming here, I'd only urinated two or three times — and that was only at the very start. I hadn't taken a shit even once.

There'd been guys grumbling that they were sweating so much they couldn't even piss, and I could barely remember seeing anyone head out to relieve themselves.

"And look, everyone here looks younger than their age, right?"

"……? Yeah, now that you mention it."

"Same as nobles. Apparently aging slows down."

Once it was explained, it all clicked into place. Nobles lived far longer than commoners. I'd heard it wasn't just from eating well — the noble bloodline itself was the reason — and I'd heard a thing or two about mana at the tavern.

Still, I'd thought it had nothing to do with me. *Must be nice,* I'd thought, half-listening, letting it go in one ear and out the other — never imagining any of it would apply to me.

"Once you can move your body with mana, you can exert force on a completely different level from muscle, and the fatigue is much less. Once you learn it, it's just a matter of getting used to it……if you consciously move your body using that sensation instead of muscle, you'll probably be able to move like Mia before long."

"Got it. Thanks."

"Well, probably in a few more days the captain'll tell everyone anyway. Seems like once about half have figured it out, they move to the next stage……and from what I can tell, about a third have caught on already."

Just as Kalua said, three days later the captain gathered us in the warehouse. For the first time, the unit's founding purpose was revealed — a special light-infantry unit composed entirely of mana wielders — and we received an explanation of mana and physical augmentation.

Those who hadn't figured it out yet looked astonished, but those like Koza who had already grasped it and been hiding it nodded along in understanding.

The training wouldn't change much for a while yet, but from now on, those who'd learned would be tasked with helping those who hadn't — that was the directive.

There was even a small reward for the first squad whose members all learned to manipulate mana. Squads that fell behind had always been saddled with the cleanup duties of the failing squads, but it was around this point that the system shifted from punishment to reward.

It probably meant the period of pure suffering was over, but at the time we were all overjoyed. Put it in words and it's just the old carrot and stick, but you really can't underestimate it. From the perspective of those actually going through it, it was a carrot dangled before men who were worn out in body and soul.

We'd been given a rest day as well, and from the next day on, everyone threw themselves into it.

Looking back with feet on the ground, it wasn't that much money, but the kind of men who became soldiers were all living hand to mouth. Even a few silver coins as a reward on top of regular pay was cause for celebration.

Koza was no exception, and as one of the forerunners who'd already learned to handle mana, he coached his squadmates. He was actually toward the back of the pack among those who'd clearly mastered it, but perhaps the struggle had made him a better teacher. Koza's squad was the first to achieve it, and they were praised before everyone.

By the time mana wielders made up the majority, we were called in for individual evaluations.

"Mm……as a squad leader, you pass."

"Indeed — Koza has a sharp eye. With further training, he could amount to something."

After Krische-sama explained the unit's purpose once more, we were called in one by one and asked basic questions about tactics. None of us knew the first thing about tactics; the questions were geared more toward testing imagination and quickness of thought.

At the time, Krische-sama was, to us, an evil noblewoman. Being told "you pass" in that condescending way grated, but no one was about to pick a fight with someone the captain showed deference to.

The captain was a good man by any measure — a model soldier. Exactly the kind of man you'd picture when you heard "centurion" — yet when it came to Krische-sama, he was almost excessively careful.

Was even a fine man like that weak before power, or was that simply what soldiers were? We didn't know Krische-sama's true ability at the time, and since we had too much exposure to her, we were in fact skeptical of her strength. Even when we heard rumors she was a monster, most men laughed it off.

Every time the captain bowed and scraped before Krische-sama, it left a bad taste in our mouths.

From there the unit was reorganized, and we moved on to combat-simulation training in the forest. Having been subjected to nothing but hard labor, we were thrilled.

And what we discovered in that training was genuinely moving. In barely a month, the unit had transformed into something entirely different.

For a while our bodies were thrown off balance by the unfamiliar augmentation, but once we could wield it properly — the potential was staggering. Men like me who'd done rough work before could quit the military and go back to our old trade as top-tier hands with offers pouring in. I was certain I could take on several pre-augmentation versions of myself and send them packing.

And the crowning moment was a live-exercise bout against two light-infantry centuries selected for the reds. Two centuries — double our number — and we crushed them.

Their side was nearly wiped out; our side had seven confirmed kills.

Light infantry was the army's finest. Men chosen as "reds" were already proven swordsmen, and having overwhelmed them, we were ecstatic.

When the captain told us that Krische-sama would personally assess our abilities next, we went wild. The cruder fellows jabbered about ripping the mad rabbit's clothes in a staged accident, or beating her senseless and making her cry — the usual nonsense.

Koza was, if anything, on that side of things — I at least wanted to humiliate her and vent the frustration we'd built up — and most of us probably felt the same. That's how brutal Krische-sama's training had been. Even looking back now, I'd say the same, so there's no helping it.

But our opponent was none other than Krische-sama.

There's always someone better, and people are not created equal, and reality is a ruthless thing.

The very next day, we were made to understand that we were nothing more than nobodies with a little extra hair on them.

What is the most unreasonable thing in life?

If someone asked me that, I'd tell them this:

Being made to stand with a sword in your hand before the princess named Krische Christand. That's what.

# Chapter 333: The Dawn Crescent — Two

The next day, the young lady of the Christand household arrived at the forest training ground same as always. True to the rumor that she hated horses, she'd walked all the way there, wearing that listless expression.

The captain barked out a crisp salute and we followed suit; she returned it with barely a glance.

I'd thought she might at least congratulate us on our decisive victory — but of course, no such thing happened.

"Well, let's get started. Krische wouldn't mind taking you all on at once, but……Dagra, what do you think?"

"Ma'am. If possible, I'd ask that you work with us one squad at a time. I believe that would be easier for the men to learn from."

"Mm, I see……"

With that, Krische-sama turned her eyes on us for the first time. Probably because I happened to be nearest, she looked at me and beckoned.

"Second Squad, on the double! Everyone else, step back."

Dagra called out the orders, and in the cleared space, it was the five of us facing Krische-sama.

At that moment, I think I was still assuming she meant to have us do practice swings or something.

"Dagra, wooden sword."

"Ma'am."

The captain immediately presented his wooden sword to Krische-sama with both hands, then stepped back.

Spinning the wooden sword deftly in one hand, she said to me:

"Go ahead."

"……? 'Go ahead' meaning?"

"Koza! Answer like a soldier!"

"Y-yes, sir! My apologies, Captain! What does 'go ahead' mean, ma'am?!"

I hastily rephrased after the captain's bark, and Krische-sama tilted her head slightly.

*What is he even asking?* — that sort of look, as she traced her lips with a fingertip, thinking.

If anyone should have been tilting their head, it was us. The idea that she was ordering us to attack her five against one — such an insane command — hadn't even crossed our minds, and we exchanged glances.

"As I told you yesterday, the corps commander's adjutant will personally be putting you through your paces from now on. If you understand, draw your swords!"

"Y-yes, sir……wait, ma'am? You mean five against one?"

"That's right. ……My apologies, adjutant. Is that correct?"

"Yes. Krische was being unclear. ……So with that understood, whenever you're ready."

Even hearing it, confusion was the dominant feeling. We'd been told about this, true, but five against one — and our opponent was a child not even fifteen.

We'd talked big the day before, but having five grown men gang up on her felt a bit much.

"It's five against one, but you don't need to hold back. Come at Krische like you mean to kill her. Um, even if Krische gets hurt or dies, Krische swears on her name she won't complain."

She nodded to herself, matter-of-fact, and we exchanged glances again.

*What do we do?* — the others looked at me with their eyes, and with no other choice I drew the wooden sword from my belt and raised it.

"Hurting her is going too far."

I whispered it, and *right*, they nodded.

"Krische is saying you don't need to hold back, though……"

She shouldn't have been able to hear at that distance and volume, but what kind of ears did she have?

In hindsight, it was probably lip-reading. Krische-sama had frighteningly good eyes and could read words from the movement of lips alone.

"Well, fine. It's the first time, after all — let's get through this quickly, explanations and all."

Five ken between us and her.

It wasn't that I couldn't see it. I saw the moment Krische-sama lowered her stance. I saw the moment she lunged.

I just saw it, was all.

How to describe that strange sensation — it's difficult. I knew she'd lunged. My head understood I needed to block. But my body felt no danger whatsoever, and didn't so much as twitch.

Usually it was the opposite. If someone suddenly slashed at you, your body moved before your brain could think — instinctively pulling back, creating distance.

You didn't need to be a veteran of rough work. Even a complete amateur would flinch if someone jumped out from a shadow. Whether you froze or your legs gave out varied by person, but the body moved before thought.

That's how human beings are wired — or so I'd believed.

And yet, there I stood with my wooden sword raised, watching dumbly as it was batted away, not reacting until a kick to the gut sent me tumbling.

I grimaced through the pain and looked up — all four of my squadmates were on their knees. Only Krische-sama was standing.

"Something like that. Krische is strong, so it's fine. Next squad."

"Oi, Second Squad's in the way. Drag them aside."

And so, unable to recover from the pain and confusion, we were hauled to the edge of the ring.

Thanks to our sacrifice, the next squad was at least granted the chance to strike first — but their wooden swords didn't so much as graze her.

Three squads were routed in quick succession without landing a single blow, and the smirking faces of the men watching — *what are those idiots doing?* — grew grim.

Krische-sama's movements showed ample reserve. To an observer, she seemed only slightly faster than normal, and the squads that hadn't gone yet might have thought they could manage where we couldn't — yet somehow, everyone's reactions were delayed.

They stood in a daze as their swords were knocked away before they even realized she'd closed the gap, or their heads were cracked, or they were kicked sprawling. The members of this unit — the same men who had crushed the reds — looked no better than training scarecrows.

Kirik, the second-best swordsman in the unit after Kalua, was toyed with as easily as one would handle a child. The First Squad, picked from the best fighters aside from the adjutant Mia, was dispatched just as effortlessly.

In no time, forty men — without so much as clashing wooden swords, without landing a touch — were on their knees before a single person.

*Taking you all on at once would be fine.*

Only now did everyone finally understand what those words had meant.

Even if every person in this unit came at her at once, defeating them would be trivial. That was the intent behind those words.

No one was laughing anymore. The air had turned to ice.

In a real battle, all forty of us — myself included — would be dead at Krische-sama's hands. And if she felt like it, she could undoubtedly massacre everyone here.

If five couldn't even provoke a reaction, then whether it was dozens or hundreds made no difference.

Cutting down hundreds with nothing but a small curved sword meant for self-defense, a monster no seasoned warrior could match — we'd all heard the ridiculous rumors and laughed them off.

If it were some hulking strongman of inhuman stature, the stories might have been believable. But a child not even five shaku tall? No matter what, that was absurd.

Even if sycophants around the Christand young lady were exaggerating to curry favor, they'd overdone it to the point of comedy — we'd laughed.

Exaggerated though they might be, rumors were rumors. She probably wasn't *weak* — we'd thought as much. But her appearance was so far removed from our image of a warrior that we'd figured at worst, she might beat us one-on-one. Maybe.

He was the biggest victim, you might say. Truly pitiable.

Unable to say much to Beltz in his quiet, sentimental moment, I just clapped him on the shoulder.

If the birth of the world's strongest private unit was the day Krische-sama showed us her sword, then the reason Beltz and I ended up running a restaurant together was, without a doubt, that single word: *delicious*.

Showing us the sword was simply instruction. The word *delicious* was simply an honest reaction to drinking the soup. For Krische-sama, there probably wasn't any great thought behind either — she just did what came to mind, said what she felt, and that was that.

But for most other people, it's never just that.

Krische-sama's unreasonableness wasn't limited to the sword.

Her very existence was likely made of the word "unreasonable."

A single offhand action, a single word — and she'd break or derail someone's life, or change it — never noticing, never knowing, munching cookies with that easy expression.

The supreme embodiment of every natural disaster — that must be what they call Krische Christand.

I think so, from time to time.

# Chapter 334: The Dawn Crescent — Three

Krische-sama had several ominous epithets, but the most famous was surely "the Head-Huntress."

Later, the modifier naturally fell away and the name stood alone, but I haven't forgotten its origin.

Or rather, for us, it's more accurate to say it was unforgettable.

*'The objective is to buy time until allied forces can regroup. Therefore, disruption operations against the enemy take highest priority. Limit engagements to ambushes and raids. Unless separately instructed, prepare to withdraw once the scouts have been dealt with. Do not pursue without orders.'*

Those words marked the start of a week that felt like an eternity.

The struggle for the Dragon's Maw ended in victory for Prince Gildanstein.

Krische-sama — the kind of monster even the fearsome prince had fled from.

A monster among monsters who'd gone alone up the cliffside and retrieved the general's sword.

A monster who'd do anything to win was our monster, not the enemy's.

Most of us probably assumed it was over after that raid. By any standard, the results were praiseworthy for a single century.

Ordinary soldiers learned basic earthwork, sure, but the engineers who could actually construct a fort were carpenters and craftsmen by trade — without them, repairs would take ages.

"Prepare for a long rest while we wait for the adjutant to return. Squads One through Ten, lie down in the center — you may sleep lightly. Everyone else, sit along the perimeter, facing outward. ……Korintz, post sentries."

Slicing through the soft flesh of necks.

*'Every hour Krische and the others buy is another hour for Selene to regroup. Krische can still fight — retreating again would mean Krische could never face Selene again.'*

*'Selene was angry at Krische for being useless. ……The head of the house died, and she cried and cried……because Krische was useless, Krische was hated by Selene……'*

When a fourteen-year-old girl, covered in wounds, fought with everything she had — grown men couldn't abandon that and run, and they couldn't utter a single complaint.

Krische-sama was unfair in so many ways.

Earnest to a fault, giving it everything, not caring what anyone thought.

She would never cry out in pain, not if her arms and legs were torn off.

And on top of it all, she looked like the most lovely princess imaginable — the kind of figure that made everyone around her feel that if *she* was going to dig in, then so would they.

Dragged along by it, forced into battles that could cost us our lives, and yet we couldn't complain. We put our lives on the line and followed.

In a sense, it was coerced.

Krische-sama simply made the people around her feel that way, without trying.

Whether she was feverish, whether she was at her limit — it didn't matter.

Krische-sama worked harder than anyone, killed more than anyone, saved more comrades than anyone.

Before a child like that, no one could have brought themselves to utter a word of complaint.

With each passing day, the situation worsened and Krische-sama's swordwork grew rougher.

What had been a sublime pinnacle of grace finally degraded to the point where even ordinary people like us could begin to grasp just how extraordinary it was.

Even mass-produced blades picked up from the ground became legendary swords of fairy tales in Krische Christand's hands.

Not a fragment of pity, not a trace of hesitation.

But it was not a human way to be.

Compared to someone who could kill people for no reason other than *they were in the way* — a pleasure killer would be a far more human creature.

Krische Christand was a girl who stepped across that line with ease.

"Tch……!"

The soldier who'd crashed into me reeked of urine.

"Captain! Orders to withdraw!"

I shouted in her stead, and the captain, noticing, responded.

The girl — the child — heard that, drifted away from me, and broke into a run——

"Ugh——……!"

Whether her legs tangled or she nearly blacked out — she stumbled, and unable to dodge a tree, slammed her left shoulder into it hard.

She crouched on the spot, clutching her left shoulder. We rushed over, and——

"……Krische is fine."

One word, then she stood as if nothing had happened and ran.

What was fine about it, none of us could tell.

*Dead people are fine too, is that it?* — we exchanged looks and ran after that wavering figure.

Even in that state, Krische-sama told us, same as always, that disruption operations would continue. She ignored the captain's strong recommendation, and fell asleep like a puppet with its strings cut.

We could say nothing, and could only watch.

It was Kalua's words that finally secured the withdrawal, finally won permission to descend the mountain.

Yet even in the moonlight, stepping out onto the grasslands, not one man wore a smile. Everyone's attention, their concern, was on Krische-sama.

"Mia, I can take over whenever."

"It's fine. She's startlingly light."

Mia answered, holding Krische-sama bundled in a blanket.

That pained sleeping face was, by any measure, not a monster or a creature — just a child of her age.

A girl so small it seemed impossible she'd been on a battlefield.

I looked at Krische-sama's beloved sword, which had been entrusted to me, and furrowed my brow.

The beautiful blade was wrecked. That it hadn't snapped was a miracle, given the countless chips and notches. Its curve rippled like a worn-out saw. Sharp chips crushed into warped scars by flesh.

The result of a single girl taking hundreds of lives.

"Koza."

Someone called me and I handed it over. Everyone who saw it wore the same furrowed brow. Anyone with the slightest experience wielding a sword could understand the abnormality.

And it wasn't simply a matter of skill or lack thereof.

The reason she'd kept using this curved sword without letting it break was that she still intended to fight.

With a body that could barely stand, she'd kept it intact to cut down tomorrow's enemies.

If no one had stopped her by force, she would have done so until she died.

She was undeniably mad.

Kalua laughed and called her mentally ill, but I agreed with every fiber.

She never abandoned anything, and she would never allow herself to.

Even words like duty or atonement became insanity past a certain line.

But Krische-sama couldn't see that line.

The general's death was not Krische-sama's fault by anyone's reckoning, and even if she bore some small part of it, she'd more than repaid it with her performance.

Yet Krische-sama blamed herself as useless, and to fulfill a responsibility that didn't exist, she wouldn't stop even if it killed her.

The worst noblewoman became the mad genius girl.

And the day she became *our* master — if I had to name it, it was surely that walk home.

After that, I saw Krische-sama fight many more times. I witnessed feats beyond belief many more times.

But that was the first and last time I saw her fight with everything she had.

Nobody said a word about it, but perhaps it was because we all felt we should.

Let this Bernaich be the last.

——As swords of Krische Christand.

Words too embarrassing to ever actually speak aloud, and I never did — but this much I can say with certainty.

From that day forward, we all carried the same words in our hearts. Of that, I am sure.

# Chapter 335: The Dawn Crescent — Four

We had done the right thing — a small mutiny born entirely of goodwill.

But put it into words, and it was seizing command and retreating without authorization.

Everyone knew we were probably in trouble.

Three days later, training resumed — and not a soul complained. Everyone threw themselves into it earnestly.

Of course, the crucible of real combat played its part. The loss of comrades, too. But more than anything, it was Krische-sama.

That battle had given us a hazy outline of who Krische-sama was as a person, but at the same time, it had eliminated any room to doubt how terrifying she was.

For Krische-sama, killing people was no more than mowing grass. Enemies were talking weeds, and she showed no mercy, no hesitation, no pity.

Something was fundamentally different in the way her mind worked.

Most of us — myself included — had our share of experience. I'd seen my share of merciless villains. But I'd never seen a human being take lives with such utter indifference.

Our actions had been goodwill, true. But whether that young lady would see it that way was highly doubtful.

Naturally, we'd also done our part in battle. We wouldn't be executed — we were fairly sure of that. But in our hearts, we'd already worked out an escape plan, "just in case."

When Krische-sama arrived about a week later——

*'Krische has various complaints, but this matter will be dropped.'*

——those words brought relief from the bottom of our hearts.

And when we were told there would even be rewards, every last man erupted in joy.

Given the scale of our achievements, we deserved at least that much — but we'd half-expected to be killed for it. Rewards instead of punishment? Of course we were in high spirits.

We got wrapped up in discussing what our unit emblem should look like — I jumped in too, arguing back and forth.

Nearly half the unit participated, everyone pitching ideas.

The density of shared time, you might call it. Going through battle together closes distances fast.

People have their preferences, and there's always one or two you don't get along with. But the kind of man you'd never share a drink with if you met him in town — that was the kind of man you could throw an arm around and laugh with, once he was a comrade-in-arms.

And the century had all kinds, for better or worse.

Farmers, men who'd never held a sword — they naturally clustered with their own kind, and bouncer types like me gravitated to our own. It was only natural when you didn't have anything to talk about.

Before the battle, the groups had been fairly segregated, but that changed completely after.

The battlefield reveals who you are. The blowhard who'd always strutted around turned out to be timid underneath, so nervous he charged in panting and tripped over his own feet. The scrawny, meek kid everyone had dismissed ran in and shielded him.

It was that harsh a battle, and every man owed his life to someone.

When the bully and the bullied threw arms over each other's shoulders and laughed, I thought — *this isn't bad at all.*

I'd had this foolish pride — the lone wolf who'd carved his way through the world on nothing but his fists. A cut above the slack-jawed faces around me, a man who didn't run with the pack. And if I died in some gutter one day, that'd be a fitting end for a life lived my way. Not a bad life, I'd thought.

But if I really believed that with all my heart, why did a man like me ever think of joining the army?

Looking back now, I think I must have been drawn, unconsciously, to that kind of circle.

The others were the same. There were plenty of men just like me.

Making friends for one night over drinks, buying love for one night with coin — both dissolving into nothing at dawn. Yesterday and today were different worlds, yet every day was the same.

Couldn't see a month ahead; thinking about the future dragged you down; numbed the ache with whatever throwaway distraction was at hand.

We all wanted something solid under our feet. That's why we'd gathered here.

Put through nonsensical training under a genius mad-rabbit commander. Cursing every single day, training to the point of death, fighting.

But then — the uproar over deciding a single unit emblem.

*What are we even getting so worked up about?* — the cool, rational part of me whispered, yet somehow, it didn't feel bad.

——How about the moon?

I don't remember who said it. Krische means the arc of the crescent moon, the person said. A well-read fellow. Probably Bilza or someone like that.

I closed my eyes and saw a moon behind my eyelids.

Freezing cold, yet undeniably lighting the world — the queen of the night.

A gentle guide, illuminating the road ahead through the dark.

A waning crescent about to disappear, and the girl from the other day overlapped with it — and behind her, the faint shapes of us, following in her wake.

*Then the banner's got to be black,* someone said. Those who march beneath the moon — the Black Century is the night that surrounds it.

*Not bad,* everyone laughed and nodded.

* * *

It was after the battle with Klare Markelus.

Kalua brought the princess to a victory celebration we'd thrown on our own initiative.

I'd always known the woman was brazen, but this was fearless beyond measure. Carried in Kalua's arms, Krische-sama looked as though she found the whole thing a nuisance. Set down on Kalua's lap, she sat silently, surveying her surroundings.

Same as always — an unreadable, expressionless face.

That changed around the time one of the soldiers asked her to peel fruit.

Drinks were flowing, decorum out the window. Still, asking the commanding officer to peel fruit — what were they thinking? And yet, strangely, Krische-sama relaxed her lips, narrowed her eyes, and wore a gentle expression.

Despite being made to peel fruit, she looked like she was having the time of her life.

Krische-sama was beautiful.

Anyone who saw her thought so. Her Majesty the Queen was her spitting image, Selene-sama was beautiful too, and everyone in the Christand household — servants included — was beautiful in their own way. But Krische-sama above all was beautiful in the air she wore, in something illusory about her presence.

They say the heart shows in the face — and it's probably true. A mean person develops mean features; a timid person, a timid face.

With Krische-sama, I think her purity was what seeped out from within.

Moonlight and blazing bonfires. Soldiers illuminated in their glow. And at their center, peeling fruit — it suited her more perfectly than anything I could imagine.

That afternoon, I'd watched her cut down dozens — including a general — in the blink of an eye, but it felt like something from a dream.

The fruit-peeling session eventually ended at Kalua's word, and from there the talk turned to the enigma herself — the young lady of the Christand household, Krische Christand.

With drink flowing and cheeks flushed, tongues loosened. Everyone was eager to hear what she'd say. Morbid curiosity, you might call it.

Less than a year since her first battle. Yet by then she'd likely killed over a thousand with her own hands, taken that many lives, and made tens of thousands of their families, lovers, and friends suffer.

Of course people were curious about a girl like that.

I was no exception.

*It's war, so it can't be helped* — there are plenty who say that. I usually think so too. But even so, there's a pang of guilt, the sort that surfaces when you're lying in bed at night.

*I put that man's life on a scale against my own — and was my life really worth taking his for?* Everyone's had a thought like that at some point, I'd wager.

Before joining the army especially, whenever my mood sank, thoughts like that would drift through my mind.

Killing a person means robbing a fellow human of their life. No matter how you dress it up, that fact can't be denied.

That's why people who kill need a cause, I think. For the country, for our comrades, for our families — we had to kill. So we could tell ourselves: *it was unavoidable.*

Maybe some can keep killing purely for their own sake, but they'd have to be exceptional fools or lunatics.

Normal people aren't like that. Sooner or later, it wears you down.

What interested me was Krische-sama's cause.

What cause had this girl used to justify taking over a thousand lives?

What logic allowed her to escape the guilt?

I'd already guessed it wasn't for her own sake. Anyone who'd been through Bernaich could see that. You can't sacrifice yourself that much for selfish reasons.

Mad she might be, but there had to be a reason — that was what I expected.

*'——Cleaning the manor, tending the garden, cooking with Bery and the others, eating meals, having tea……Krische wants to live like that every single day, forever.'*

But it was half a letdown.

Krische-sama wasn't a decent human being who fought under some banner of righteousness in the first place.

She didn't care that she'd killed a thousand. She had no interest in those she'd killed, and she didn't grant them a shred of value.

She said what she thought and acted on what she felt. When most people called her childish for that, I thought it was slightly off the mark.

Compared to us, her way of perceiving things was simply far more basic — simple enough, extreme enough, to kill a thousand without hesitation for the sake of a peaceful, happy daily life.

Honestly, it sent a chill through me.

A modest wish, certainly. Most probably found it heartbreaking and pitiable.

But flip it around, and a girl who could kill people without flinching for the sake of such a trivial happiness — that was Krische Christand. A madwoman among madwomen, nothing more.

Everything outside what concerned her own happiness was probably irrelevant to her. And anything that threatened her happiness was unforgivable.

That logic didn't exempt herself — Bernaich had been the result.

One baseless conviction — *I was useless, Selene hates me* — had driven her to the brink of death and left hundreds dead. That wasn't sane.

Some would be angry to hear Krische-sama called a madwoman, but my view hasn't changed since then. Without question, Krische-sama was abnormal, a person who belonged on that side of the line.

Someone who could render everything outside her beloved "modest daily life" worthless — if that wasn't a madwoman, no one could answer what was.

Watching Krische-sama, I sometimes thought: *love, at its core, is discrimination.*

Wanting to be good to someone, wanting to make someone happy — no one would fault those feelings. But the world is complicated. Making one person happy can mean making another miserable.

A decent person weighs the scales in their mind each time, measuring what matters more. But Krische-sama probably didn't agonize over such things.

For her, everything was clear-cut: one or zero.

Simple, extreme, without hesitation.

I've never known a person as full of love as Krische-sama.

I've never known anyone as sincere, as earnest, as pure.

And I feel it, honestly — there's something genuinely beautiful about the way she is.

But decent folk are messier things.

They fret over how others see them, scramble not to be mocked, get tangled up in obligations, flail and struggle and live pathetic, undignified lives.

And yet, they fight to cover their own ugliness and live as best they can — that's what being human is.

A princess who could remain beautiful without ever knowing, let alone respecting, that struggle.

If someone told me *this* is the right way to be, then what were the rest of us?

That was exactly why Krische-sama was a madwoman.

"——Actually, you know, Bery can even talk to cats."

"Cats?"

"Yes. Krische was watering the orchard one day, and a cat wandered into the garden, and Bery just went up to it and said 'meow,' and started chatting. And then she translated the cat's words for Krische, and taught Krische how to talk to them too. So every time Krische sees a cat, Krische tries a little bit, but——"

No climax, no punchline.

All Krische-sama talked about was inane nonsense from the manor.

Even with drinks flowing, not a single laugh.

She had no thought for how her audience felt. She rattled off every "fun story" she could think of, one-sidedly, and never was there a worse-tasting drink.

But we — myself included, the rank and file — could only sit there and listen without complaint.

They say names reveal nature, and with Krische-sama, it was true.

A moon that illuminates everything equally from a place no one can reach.

If she'd stayed up in the clouds, we could have accepted it for what it was. But she insisted she was human like us, then proceeded to discriminate without batting an eye — the height of unreason.

The bonfire drowned out even the stars. Only the moon hung alone in the sky.

Around it, the sky turned indigo, repainting the dark just a little.

Without it there, the view would have been awfully lonely.

So one madwoman — that was fine.

At dawn, the moon would naturally fade and melt into the sky.

"Beltz, let's get up a little early tomorrow."

"What a coincidence. I was just thinking the same."

"Had some truly awful booze tonight. Need a palate cleanser."

"That we do."

By the time we'd been discussing the unit banner, my mind was already made up. That night was just confirmation.

To learn the reason for marching under the moon, until the dawn.

# Chapter 336: The Dawn Crescent — Five

The end of the civil war was a hard fight for us.

We knew Krische-sama's strength. We knew our own weakness. We'd survived the madness of Bernaich, and our solidarity was the strongest it had ever been.

And still, many died.

Before being reorganized into the Black Flag Special Service, on that day, we were without doubt the finest century there ever was.

Even accounting for the captain's absence, morale was at its peak. Entrusted with the princess Selene-sama, we were ready to fight with our lives on the line — literally.

Not long ago, we hadn't known the first thing about coordination, but since then, we'd fought on the front lines through three campaigns — Markelus, Hilkintos, Wirring.

On that day, we had the finest unity and courage imaginable — I'll swear to that no matter what anyone says.

But the reason monsters are called monsters is that they can overwhelm even a force like that through sheer power alone.

Through every battle until then, we'd surrounded and killed plenty of mana wielders. Among them, sure, there were men I could never have beaten one-on-one.

But if one man wasn't enough, two would do — or three, or four. Strike without mercy. Stab them in the back. That was the Black Century.

If needed, throw sand, hurl a weapon, pin them down — anything to kill.

Only the strong can afford to call something cowardly.

We knew in our bones that we were the weak ones on the battlefield, the ordinary men. Dirty, crude — but survive and a win is a win. The dead don't get to complain.

We'd refined that way of fighting, and the coordination was so smooth it didn't even require a signal.

No matter how fearsome the warrior, as a group we could always win — that was our shared conviction.

But it was just one man.

We were ready to lay down our lives. We fought dirty. We used our numbers.

And still, we couldn't kill that one man.

The giant who swung great axes in both hands — his name was Nakilus Ferizar.

Every man who stood before him had his guts scattered.

Every single one of them was a man among men. Not a hero from a fairy tale could have matched them.

The most magnificent bastards in the world.

Not a one of them had to be asked — they just stepped up, in the heat of the moment, and threw away their one and only life for their comrades.

Some said they'd go home and tell their kids *Papa's a hero.* Some said they'd propose to the girl at that shop. Some just wanted to drink until they drowned. Paltry, laughable dreams.

But to the man who held each one, it was worth more than any great cause.

And every last one of them — the monster scattered.

The world is not equal. Bundled dreams, all that brilliance — shattered by a single being's single will.

And even that monster, Krische-sama trampled without a thought.

I was relieved, but I couldn't help wondering: *what are the rest of us?*

If Krische-sama hadn't come, Mia would have died; we all might have been wiped out. I understood the logic — we'd bought time, we'd done our job.

But feelings aren't logic.

After the battle, gathering the remains of our comrades — that feeling is something only those who've been through it can understand.

The nauseating guts and severed limbs — strangely, they didn't make me sick.

*If you're gonna die, die cleaner, you bastard* — we'd grumble now and then, laughing.

*'Oy……that's my hand you've got there.'*

Even half-dead Daz was wearing the same face, laughing.

There was nothing to do but laugh.

And that night, we drank.

Not maudlin — drinking hard, raising hell, laughing.

We were never anything special to begin with. Men like me, living day to day. Failed craftsmen. Dirt farmers. Sheep herders.

Everywhere you looked, a dime a dozen — and picking up a few tricks didn't change that.

Accept it and you're back to normal.

The world is moved by a tiny handful of the few, and we weren't them. We knew that. We'd always known.

Fragile lives, no more than insects in the grand scheme.

Living in the moment, drinking, eating, raising hell — if we could laugh through it, that was enough.

And so we welcomed Krische-sama when she appeared at the feast.

We were insects, maybe, but Krische-sama wasn't.

She'd cleaved the monster Ferizar clean in two. Taken the prince's head in a single stroke when even Kalua and the others couldn't touch him.

In the paintings of this princess, we'd be somewhere in the background.

Soldiers who followed the peerless great hero. For the likes of us insects, that was more than enough — change to spare.

A victory scene should be a lively one, so the noise and revelry fit right in. We showered praise on Krische-sama's unhinged feats.

Stick with you, and we've got nothing to fear — that's what.

Sadly, though, our princess had no shortage of flaws.

*'Krische only helped out a little at the end. In that place, in that situation — even without Krische, you had the advantage, and that was thanks to this unit's performance. You all worked hard to fulfill Krische's order to protect Selene.'*

Our tone-deaf princess came out with words like that.

*'It is a shame Krische cannot say it to those who died. They disobeyed Krische's orders by dying, so Krische wanted to praise them and scold them both. ……The fools.'*

Though she'd killed people like insects, she looked down with those words.

Not laughing happily — probably picturing their absent faces, the crescent missing from the whole.

She doused cold water on our celebration, was carried off by Kalua, and left.

The feast went on after that, but one by one, men drifted away.

Enough disappeared and the feast wound down.

I went back to my cot in the tent and closed my eyes. What I heard was pathetic sobbing.

Grown men, shameful. *Somebody throw out whoever's bawling,* I thought, but instead I just turned my back on the sound.

The princess was a mass of unreason who trampled over people without a thought.

She probably never considered how those she killed had felt.

Compared to her, the monster Ferizar was more human by far.

He'd had conviction, at least. And the fight between him and us — us, the nameless insects — had been a clash of those convictions.

Even as enemies, I could respect the man for what he was. He'd been something else.

Honor, conviction, pride — we'd both fought carrying something like that, so you could accept it. *It couldn't be helped,* you could tell yourself.

We'd both fought desperately for something we couldn't bend. It wasn't just killing — it was a noble, exalted exchange of lives.

Not one of the men who died that day had any regrets.

There couldn't have been a finer place to die.

And yet, knowing nothing of what the lowly felt or why they fought, caring nothing——

That princess kicked aside stones on the road and said it.

——*It's a shame they died.*

The most obvious thing in the world, spoken without reading the room. Casually, without a thought for why no one else had said it.

As if hearing that a neighbor had met with an accident. *They were so full of life just yesterday* — that was the level of it.

Our princess, Krische Christand, did not understand the human heart.

* * *

The Black Century became the Black Flag Special Service.

Aside from nearly being stuck in a baffling outfit called the "Kuro-Fuyo Squad," there isn't much more to tell from there.

A maiden's unwanted heroic epic, carrying on until dawn.

We just ran with the moon held high.

Of course, if I sat and remembered every little thing, there'd be no end. No shortage of funny stories and stupid stories — but the truly hard times were that one stretch and no more.

Krische-sama was unreasonable and utterly without charm, and never made the same mistake twice. That era was, in a word, a stage built for Krische-sama.

The other side must have found it intolerable.

Every enemy who stood in the way was a villain destined to fall, and thanks to some cheap, absurd script, even the fiercest warrior was cut down in one stroke and sent offstage.

Krische-sama was stronger than anyone, cleverer than anyone — that kind of invincible hero.

On top of it all, as a protagonist she was third-rate, with zero interest in entertaining the audience. She crushed everything in her path, then hurried backstage to happily do behind-the-scenes work.

If I were in the audience, I'd have a few words to say — but I was cast as a soldier in her retinue. My job was to attend to the whims of a leading lady who'd rather live in the wings.

*If only this wretched show would end already,* I thought every single day.

Then the star could live backstage forever and nobody would complain. She'd never have to take the stage she didn't want, and she'd never be called to the lead role again.

Krische-sama had not one shred of interest in rank, fame, or power.

The King's Elder Sister and Alberinea. She could have sat idly and wanted for nothing — all the world would have been given to her if she'd asked — yet she preferred to play at being a servant in an apron dress.

Cold and ruthless, viewing people as less than human — and yet she'd say *working for the sake of others is a wonderful thing.* The contradiction was breathtaking.

Or perhaps, precisely because she couldn't see them as human, she wished she could.

She was called a cursed child of the royal line, and truly, Krische-sama was a madwoman.

She couldn't have killed people that mercilessly otherwise.

Krische-sama was broken at the most fundamental level — and she hated that about herself. Enough years together, and even I understood that much.

In her heart of hearts, social values probably meant nothing to her.

War or peace, a child being cut down before her eyes, entrails spilled on the ground — she could stand there with a cookie in her mouth and not bat an eye. That was who Krische-sama was.

She felt no pity, no sorrow — couldn't feel it. She simply registered what she saw — *cut down, entrails out* — and felt nothing beyond that.

And yet, once a relationship was formed, she became startlingly good-natured.

She'd find reasons to craft prosthetic limbs for her unit members. No matter what rudeness Kalua said or did, she'd at most throw a childish tantrum.

The magic weapons, too — if you traced them to their origin, they were built so that *her people* wouldn't die anymore. That was more or less the reason behind them.

With people who weren't under her command, it was even more pronounced. No matter what was done to her, she never forgot her courtesies, bowing and scraping to everyone.

She could be so good-natured it made you nervous — but only within her circle. The moment something fell outside it, she could render it all worthless.

The main reason Krische-sama aspired to be a servant, I sometimes thought, was simply so she wouldn't have to show that side of herself.

A small, enclosed world where everything was *inside* and nothing lay beyond. In a place like that, Krische-sama could remain good-natured, and no one would call her mad.

Even unifying the world — that monumental achievement — probably had just that one reason behind it.

Not a princess who loved peace, but a deranged girl who needed peace just to hide the madness. And so she annihilated every enemy that stood in the way. That was all.

Some would deny it, but for me, this explanation makes the most sense.

*'——Bery said so too.'*

That was Krische-sama's signature phrase, the one everyone knew. The name that came up at every turn — Bery-sama — was her servant.

True to the Christand tradition, she was a strikingly beautiful woman, but I hadn't seen her all that often. She visited Krische-sama from time to time, but Krische-sama never once brought her before us. Her Majesty the Queen was more familiar to us, if anything.

When she did bring a servant along, it was always Kalua's younger sister, Elvena — never Bery-sama.

Grandfather Gallen-sama, former Marshal's Aide Faren, and the servant Bery-sama.

In all the world, only three people earned Krische-sama's unreserved praise — and Bery-sama especially. Whenever Bery-sama happened to appear, those two were in their own world.

Everything — conversations, training, work — was dropped the instant she arrived. And at the manor, too, it was the same.

But she never brought that woman along. Why?

The answer that fit best, when I thought about it, was something embarrassingly ordinary: she didn't want to show her worse side to the woman she loved.

A perfectly normal motive anyone could understand.

Normally you'd try to change yourself so that side never showed — but changing the world instead was simply the Krische-sama brand of unreasonable.

Rather than a pure and innocent child who loved peace, she was a hopelessly troublesome maiden in love.

To me, that's who Krische-sama was — and a hopeless madwoman all the same.

* * *

——I opened my eyes, and the morning sun was blinding.

Sitting up felt like a chore. I laughed — *haven't kicked the bucket yet, have I?*

Looking out the window, there was that preposterous tree in full bloom, its flowers shimmering in rainbow hues.

Utterly inconsiderate of anyone around it — the sheer scale was Krische-sama all over.

I wondered how many people could understand, if told this was built as a love nest to hide away in. But anyone who *could* understand was as mad as she was.

The boys had come in a panic — was it last night?

In the sky above, a faint crescent moon was visible.

My body felt remarkably well.

Might as well show my face at the restaurant, I thought, and stood.

The old man in the mirror looked like he could drop dead at any moment. Bald on the forehead and crown — I was in no position to laugh at the captain.

Though the captain had just shaved his head. He hadn't actually been bald. Probably.

Scratching at my unkempt beard, I splashed water from the basin on my face and stepped into the hall.

An overly grand, long corridor. Thanks to generous fellows who said they'd pay in booze, we'd ended up with a handsome three-story inn, but in old age, it wasn't the most convenient.

Getting older meant being shunted to the far end of the second floor, which made the corridor feel even longer.

Lively voices drifted up from the first floor. Today's crowd was a good one, by the sound of it.

"Pops, are you sure about this? Up and walking all of a sudden?"

Coming up the stairs was an old woman, her tone exasperated.

The daughter who'd once had skin like porcelain was now a wrinkled, nagging old crone.

*We've gotten old,* I laughed, and leaned on her shoulder.

"Stairs are too much trouble. I want to eat downstairs for once. Take me."

"……Honestly. And if you fall on your own?"

"Fine way for me to die, wouldn't you say."

She sighed. I laughed and let her bring me down.

The spacious dining floor was full of patrons, all buzzing about that tree.

At the counter, a few old men from the neighborhood sat drinking from the morning.

"What, Tagel, you're still alive too?"

"Look who's talking. I heard you were bedridden."

"Probably dead by tomorrow."

*Here,* I said to my daughter, and took the seat next to Tagel.

The honored sergeant of the Black Century. He'd always been a handsome man, and even on death's doorstep as an old codger, that hadn't changed. The neatly trimmed beard was so like him.

"I felt the same way, but today I woke up in great shape."

"What a coincidence. For the first time in a while, even the mana's cooperating."

"That tree's probably the reason……but either way, it won't last long. While I'm still feeling good, I thought I'd have one last meal here."

Tagel soaked bread in the lamb stew and popped it into his mouth. The scent that tickled my nose wasn't half bad.

A bit heavy for a morning soup, but the herbs gave it a fresh finish.

*Made it the way I taught them,* I thought with satisfaction, nodding.

"Kuuri, same for me."

"You got it."

My daughter set a glass of water in front of me and headed for the kitchen.

"I thought I wouldn't wake up this morning. But I spent last night remembering old times, and it wasn't a bad feeling."

"Think of it as Krische-sama's gracious consideration — letting you see your own shop one last time before you die. We've lived long enough, after all."

"Optimistic bastard. Can't stand it."

"I intend to enjoy every last moment. Once I've painted that tree, I can die happy."

Listening to him, I tilted the glass and drank. Behind me, idiotic laughter — what was there to be so happy about this early in the morning?

Drinking from the morning — a pack of good-for-nothings.

I took Tagel's wine bottle and poured some into my emptied glass.

"You'll catch hell for that."

"You're sneaking drinks behind your family's back too. At least I'm doing it out in the open."

"Oh, by this evening, someone from here will be bringing bread over and will be sure to tell one of mine. I'm drinking in full expectation of the lecture."

I eyed the smiling Tagel and brought the wine to my lips.

Cheap wine, but not bad. When it's been long enough, anything tastes fine.

"Well, no quarreling. I want to enjoy my last drink in peace."

"Agreed. For the first time in a while, I'm in the mood for some old stories."

"When you're this old, what else is there to talk about?"

I held out my glass toward the bottle in Tagel's hand.

"……The moon's faded away somewhere. A fine time for that kind of talk."
