# Chapter 361: In a Far, Far Future IV

"……I am not at all convinced!"

"Now, now…… Kreschenta-sama, it's just a recreational game."

The place was what they called the Second Manor — a villa.

They preferred to do everything by hand, cherishing the old ways, but they did have a certain interest in the march of progress.

The world beyond theirs had produced all manner of inventions through cycles of advancement and decline, and to ignore all of that and cling solely to the old way of life would be unhealthy.

On the other hand, adopting every invention indiscriminately risked abandoning the traditional lifestyle they loved — and so what they had devised was the Second Manor.

The First Manor, where they normally spent their time, preserved their existing way of life.

But in this Second Manor, they freely embraced the inventions of the outside world and enjoyed the fruits of human progress.

Washing machines and vacuum cleaners were the least of it; the vast garden was paved into a circuit.

Cars and motorcycles, drones and airboards that raced through the air were all lined up, and from time to time they would hold the Mansion Grand Prix, which even Gururun could enjoy.

Inside the building, computing machines for every conceivable form of entertainment had been gathered from around the world — to call it a manor was inaccurate; "large-scale amusement facility" was more fitting.

The Third Recreation Room of the Second Manor.

Projected in the center of the room was the game screen of *Forgotten Alberan 7*, the latest installment of the strategy simulation series *Chronicles of Alberan*.

A simulation game set in that era, it featured a simplified yet realism-focused in-game economy.

On the battlefield, each soldier was individually controlled, and whether playing as a common foot soldier, a commanding officer, or a ruler, the experience adapted to one's play style — it was a talked-about, hardcore game.

The series had apparently been running for about two thousand years, and the company behind it had released several other games recreating the world of that era.

Sitting on the sofa was a red-haired servant — and sitting atop her was the ancient queen Kreschenta (the real one), clad in a white one-piece dress, gripping her controller with a displeased expression.

What she was glaring at was the ancient queen Kreschenta (the fake one) displayed there on screen.

More precisely, she was examining the displayed ability scores, a furrow deepening between her brows.

"My ability scores are clearly far too low relative to Ojii-sama's. My political ability is so low that even Selene-sama is closing in on it. How anyone could arrive at such a conclusion is beyond me."

"……You really are rude, you know. Well, I'll admit I do feel a bit overestimated in some areas…… But conversely, Otou-sama is a bit underestimated."

Selene, sitting beside her and also gripping a controller, said with a touch of dissatisfaction.

"It has been ages, after all. It can't be helped. Margrave Agrand and Margrave Velraigh, who were splendid warriors, seem to have been properly evaluated…… Rather, it's more than I expected. Look, they've even compiled individual anecdotes…… If anything, I'm impressed."

"……There's an anecdote here that I have absolutely no memory of, though. 'In his final years, he cut down an assassin while still seated in his wheelchair' — what on earth kind of situation is that?"

"Fufu, such is the nature of history."

Bery shook her shoulders with amusement.

"Long ago, I had some resistance to this sort of thing…… but after this many years, that feeling has faded considerably. The people depicted in this game are merely how people of the current era imagine those of that time — it's only natural they'd be different from the real thing."

"Well, true…… There is a sense of 'this again' at this point. That drama a while back had you as the final villain, and I couldn't help laughing…… In a way, it wasn't wrong, though."

"That one was quite the novel take…… Krische-sama was also a magical puppet that I had created."

She smiled wryly, and nuzzled her cheek against the head of Krische, who was clinging to her left arm and sleeping.

"However…… For something set over 15,000 years in the past, it does seem a bit too accurate."

"Too accurate……?"

Bery tilted her head at the voice of Elvena, who was similarly holding a controller.

"I have occasionally been reviewing the historical accounts regarding that era…… After the great geological event, when so many historical materials were lost, records pertaining to Alberan diminished considerably. The key figures were still documented, of course, but……"

At one point, the inhabitants of this planet had been brought to the brink of extinction.

A massive geological upheaval — simultaneous volcanic eruptions and a winter of ash.

For a time, humans could not survive outside of mana shelters, and here too, they had debated several times whether to intervene on their behalf.

If they wished it, Krische or Kreschenta could have resolved everything with ease.

But there was also a strong view that so long as their paradise was not threatened, they should not interfere.

Having stepped outside the circle of the human world, they should maintain a minimum of propriety.

They concluded that without the resolve to truly become gods, they ought not meddle carelessly.

Before long, the World Tree — apparently responding to humanity's unconscious wishes — had gradually cleared the ash covering most of the planet, and they were relieved. Still, for several decades, everyone wore somewhat dark expressions.

*"This planet is my garden. It is the duty of my subjects to keep the garden clean, so that it pleases my eye and I may walk through it in comfort."*

After that, a single remark from Kreschenta — the first time Her Majesty the Queen had made her voice heard in thousands of years — led to a significant lowering of the threshold.

In the case of natural disasters beyond the capacity of the people of the time — in short, the great queen's decree that "if you're going to make gloomy faces fretting over such trifles, just quietly help them in secret" — they had thereafter tightened their surveillance and settled on a policy of preventing major disasters before they occurred. But that event had caused civilization to decline drastically.

Much knowledge was lost from the people, and materials with little practical value — historical records in particular — suffered all the more.

The once-unified calendar diverged once again, and records predating that fragmentation were increasingly treated with skepticism, marked off as unreliable.

From their perspective, it was simply a loss of knowledge; the history was clearly continuous.

But from the perspective of the people living in that era, it was a very different matter.

Just as the great empire of Lusheran, which had once flourished and been destroyed by Kushenalars, had been mere fairy-tale legend to them.

Having declined to the point where even maintaining simple magical devices was a struggle, the notion that a magical civilization capable of mass-producing such things had once existed must have seemed no more than a mythological dream.

The long years it took, after the winter of ash ended, to reclaim what had been lost.

With most primary sources gone, they had watched firsthand as their own era transformed into myth and legend with each passing generation — history blending with fiction.

"I believe that discussion was the cause, but after the Minalshi arrived, a large-scale review of the historical materials relating to Alberan and Kleinmeil was conducted, and whatever remained at the time should have been thoroughly combed through. It's hard to imagine new materials being discovered at this point."

"……That's true. Given the situation, the Minalshi would have investigated seriously."

When the Minalshi arrived, there had been a bit of a commotion.

Krische had jumped to conclusions, mistaking them for a threat to destroy the planet, and their existence was revealed, causing considerable alarm — ultimately resulting in their mediating a peace between the Minalshi and the amoeba-like beings called the Arnia.

She picked up the squirmy Unyoon sleeping inside the pouch at Krische's waist and smiled wryly.

The Minalshi had dutifully upheld the condition that they forget about them and never involve themselves again, and even now the manor remained peaceful and undisturbed.

It was likely in no small part because they feared Krische's absurd power — but whatever the case, during that period, investigating them would have been a matter of urgency.

There was no doubt they had gathered and examined every available historical record pertaining to the ancient nation of Alberan, and that would have formed the basis for current historical accounts.

"You're saying that the descriptions here are unnaturally accurate?"

"Yes. There are several individuals recorded here who, as far as current records go, should not be known. I may be misremembering, of course, but……"

"……Now that you mention it, you're right. I certainly don't remember every single person, but there are names here of people who didn't distinguish themselves on the battlefield, and that is a little unnatural."

Selene frowned as she examined the commander units projected on screen.

"Elvena-sama does have quite a good memory. If you say so, then it must be so. The names of civilian officials are made up, but when it comes to nobles who held military rank, most of them were real people…… I had assumed records that detailed had simply survived."

That is a bit curious, Kreschenta said, and turned her eyes to the sleeping Krische.

Bery, Selene, and Elvena all found their gazes drifting toward Krische as well, somewhat instinctively.

When something happened at the manor, Krische was usually the cause.

"Mm……ghh!?"

Selene unceremoniously pinched Krische's cheek, and Krische, who had been sleeping blissfully, jolted awake.

"S-Selene……?"

"It's obviously you, isn't it? I know it."

"……? Wh-what are you talking…… oww."

"Now, now…… you mustn't bully her."

That's right, Kreschenta said, glaring at Selene, as she waved a finger.

The light of mana slipped inside the recreation computing machine — and Kreschenta's brow furrowed deeply.

"Is something wrong?"

"There was encrypted data sleeping inside it. Crude work, but it's an application of primordial inscription — rare in this day and age."

My, Bery said in surprise, tracing the path Kreschenta had opened and channeling her own mana through it.

Far from crude, the encrypted text had been woven into the system portion designed for mana-operated experiential play, concealed with a high degree of sophistication.

The people of today, who knew only the four-image dual-element inscriptions, would never even notice it.

Reading the inscribed text, the servant cocked her head.

"……'I would like to meet you, Master.'"

What could that mean? She swayed her red hair, looking puzzled.

* * *

"A game developer, of all things……"

"I'm sorry…… No, I did think it was a stretch myself, but the timing was just too perfect."

In Doguru's drawing room, beside a sheepish Miri scratching her cheek, a black-haired girl was bowing deeply.

A classmate from childhood and Miri's best friend — Saria, she was called.

She had apparently visited Ols's house many times over the years, had known Sena for a long time, and now that it was mentioned, Doguru did have a vague memory of seeing her with Miri once before he turned himself in.

"……I understand the situation. I swear that I will treat Sena-san purely as a collaborator in game development and will never divulge her origins, and I am willing to sign a written oath if necessary. Of course, if you are still concerned even so, Doctor, I will give up, and I intend to carry what I heard today to my grave."

"I understand how you feel. Well, please raise your head."

The bespectacled girl who looked up at his words was visibly tense.

She was apparently quite the gaming enthusiast, and Miri had recently been invited by her to make a game together.

The subject was a simulation game set in the ancient Kingdom of Alberan.

Alberan, as the birthplace of the subsequent magical civilization, was a popular era even among history buffs, and that had likely been true before the near-extinction event as well.

The nation of the divine children, preserved in countless plays, stories, and legends.

She was an avid fan of that era.

Miri, as the daughter of a major corporation's president, had apparently also harbored a desire to create something on her own without relying on her parents' influence.

She had agreed to the idea, but when confronted with the enormous volume of data that Saria demanded, she had been at a loss — until just the other day, when she learned Sena's origins, and impulsively…… or so the story went.

Ols had scolded Miri in exasperation, asking if she understood how delicate this matter was, and she was on the verge of tears — but Sena herself was her usual self.

*"If it is Miri-sama's wish, I have no particular objection."*

She had said just that.

Sena was like that about most things.

Even when the conversation was about her, she seemed uninterested; right now too, she sat beside them listening while cracking apart cookies.

Her usual stance was: just tell her the conclusion.

Of course, if asked her opinion she would state her view, but unless addressed directly — aside from correcting pronunciations — she remained silent.

When told, "We want to keep your origins hidden, because of various complications," she complied without protest; she seemed uninterested in most things, including herself.

Repaying favors, and fulfilling requests in order to repay them — those two things were her everything.

And yet, precisely because of that, her words had been somewhat unexpected.

The first on her "repayment priority list" was presumably Doguru, and the second was Ols.

By anyone's reckoning, Ols's scolding had been reasonable, and his opinion prioritized Sena's safety.

If she were making a mechanical, rational judgment, deferring to Ols's opinion would have been appropriate, and normally she would not have intervened.

But Sena had glanced at Miri's face, on the verge of tears, and then spoken up.

The same emotionless expression as always, the same face, the same tone of voice.

The reason Doguru had said that it might be worth hearing them out was that her words had struck him as unexpected.

Inexplicably, somehow.

Because the fact that he found it unexpected felt, in itself, terribly important.

"What do you want to do, Sena?"

"As I stated earlier…… If Doctor Chestnut-Head grants permission, I have no particular reason to refuse Miri-sama's request."

When asked, Sena tilted her head and answered, and Doguru followed up.

"Why did you decide to go along with Miri-chan's request?"

"Why?"

"Ols-san was prioritizing your safety above all, and I thought his scolding was reasonable. You did too, didn't you, Miri-chan?"

"Huh? Oh, yes…… I was clearly in the wrong."

Miri said awkwardly, and Doguru smiled wryly — he wasn't blaming anyone.

"It seemed to me that you said what you did out of concern for Miri-chan being scolded."

"……?"

"It looked to me like you spoke up because Miri-chan was about to cry. Am I wrong?"

*That is correct,* Sena answered, and Miri's eyes went wide.

"For example, if Miri-chan had been perfectly fine — would you have interjected the same way against Ols-san's scolding?"

"No. Miri-sama being on the verge of tears was a factor in my judgment at that point."

"Why?"

"When Miri-sama starts crying, it increases the amount of trouble."

At those words Miri froze, and Doguru smiled wryly.

"Then let me ask this. What do you think about Ols-san scolding Miri-chan? Was it an unreasonable reprimand?"

"No. I consider it a justified reprimand that prioritized the concealment of my origins."

"Exactly. In essence, Miri-chan was being a little selfishly willful and received a deserved scolding. Weighed against the risk of your origins being exposed, the inconvenience of Miri-chan crying is a minor matter. And yet you tried to shield her."

Doguru nodded.

"Don't you think that was an irrational decision?"

He smiled at her as he said it.

"……Irrational."

"If your origins are exposed, it causes trouble not just for you, but for me, Ols-san, and many other people. And yet you accepted — however slight — an increase in that risk, solely because Miri-chan was upset."

Sena furrowed her brow at his words, as if deep in thought.

Saria spoke up in a fluster.

"U-um, Doctor…… This is just a game development proposal, and if you say no, really——"

"Ah, sorry. I'm not blaming you or Sena. In fact, I'd like to welcome your proposal."

"Um, what do you mean by……?"

"I made a promise to her. What I said just now relates to that."

It had also been one of the reasons he had left Sena with Ols.

For better or worse, Doguru was an adult living alone now; he had thought Sena would gain more from spending time with Miri, a child raised in a loving family, than from being around him.

Sena was extraordinarily capable, but emotionally she was like a child.

He had felt that showing her what children and family looked like, even once, would be good for her.

He was fairly sure that judgment had been right.

"Any objections, Sena?"

"……I believe it was a reasonable decision within the priorities and criteria that exist within me. But as you say, I also believe it was not a rational decision."

Tilting her head, Sena said with a puzzled air:

"It is difficult to understand."

"It is difficult to understand."

Doguru laughed, looking pleased.

"Still, I think that realization is something very precious."

"……Precious."

"Humans are far more irrational creatures than you might think. They avoid a small disadvantage right in front of them at the cost of accepting a much larger one down the road, or they let go of a great benefit entirely. You could chalk it up to intelligence, but I don't think that's it."

For example, he said, picking up two cookies.

"You have brethren — others born with the same intelligence and capabilities. But by now, they surely hold entirely different thoughts and possess different abilities. What do you think accounts for the difference?"

"I would attribute it to the accumulation of experience."

"Right. The difference lies in what kinds of realizations each has gained through experience. For instance, suppose a version of you had never refrained from fighting a red-haired woman — do you think she would have ever questioned the rule forbidding it?"

*No,* Sena answered, and Doguru nodded.

"That question was a realization unique to you. And all those various realizations have shaped who you are now. Some of your brethren may share the same ones, but you are no longer the same being as them — and humans are the same…… You have been drawing steadily closer to the answer you're looking for."

"……Is that really so?"

"Yes. I believe so."

Noting the bewildered expressions of the two girls, Doguru laughed.

"If you know your Alberan…… you must also know why those units, which once dominated every battlefield, were captured by the coalition?"

"Y-yes."

"Sena has always wondered why Alberinea gave them such a weakness."

The two stared at each other in round-eyed surprise.

"When I heard that, I thought she should find the answer on her own. Because knowing something and understanding it are similar but different things."

As he spoke, he placed a cookie in Sena's hand.

"The promise was about that — I told her I'd help her find it. And on that note, I also thought your proposal to create a game, an entertainment, would be a good thing for her. ……Because the act of creating something to bring others joy will give her all kinds of experiences and realizations."

He popped a cookie into his mouth.

She had apparently already mastered all sorts of recipes, but the one she made every day was cookies.

Old-fashioned, simple honey cookies.

According to Alberinea's notes, a woman named Bery had taught her that these were the most fundamental and finest cookies of all; she must have loved them dearly.

The name Bery was inscribed all throughout her evaluation criteria.

The red-haired servant who had attended Alberinea.

The Jaleia Gashea were beings born from love for that servant.

Sena had not been made to be rational in the first place.

Her judgment criteria, her behavioral principles, the poorly weighted "repayments" —

She already knew the answer and simply hadn't noticed. That was what Doguru believed.

All Doguru needed to do was provide the spark.

That alone, and eventually she would realize it.

He was certain enough of that — for she had been created with such extraordinary tenderness.

"If you're willing, I'd like both of you to help her search for that answer as well. If you agree, I'll do what I can to cooperate too."

*How about it?* he asked, and the two exchanged a glance, nodded, and bowed deeply.

Thinking about the future was a delight.

All the more so when it was the future of young people.

By some measures Doguru himself was still young, but he supposed that from the moment his beloved daughter had been born, he had become one of those who watch over others.

Not what he himself would do from now on, but how they would live their lives going forward.

He pondered such futures as they spent time together.

Eventually he would die, and Sena and the others would walk on through the world beyond.

He had been mulling over several plans for Sena's future, but life had a way of springing the unexpected.

Rather than the original plan of putting her remarkable abilities to use assisting at Ols's company, having her go along with the impulsive whims of young Miri and her friends seemed more fitting for who she was.

Alberinea remained a person he couldn't quite figure out, but somehow, watching Sena, he felt that was the kind of life she had led.

Turning a deranged war machine into a servant — sheer madness.

Cooking recipes and domestic functions crammed in on a whim.

A baffling "nickname evaluation criteria" whose purpose defied comprehension, and a mysterious obsession with demanding correct pronunciation.

*"Hey, Sena-san. I get that Jaragasha is the official name, but could you stop auto-correcting? This should be Jalei——"*

*"Jaragasha, Miri-sama."*

*"Ugh, Sena-san? Miri and I would love to use the wonderful pronunciation 'Jaragasha' too, believe me, but in terms of keeping Sena-san's origins secret, we need to use a different name, or it'll cause trouble for the Doctor and everyone."*

*"……I see."*

*She was probably a guileless, childlike person,* he thought, and it clicked.

A little stubborn, particular about things, free-spirited.

The two of them would come home from university and throw themselves into passionate debate — no, that's wrong, yes, this is right — and Sena would humor them, bringing a liveliness that reminded him of the old days.

Swept up in that energy, Doguru found himself heading to Roktarna's development division, devoting himself to mentoring the next generation of researchers.

On weekends he drank at his usual bar, sometimes with Ols joining him.

Like old times, they talked about the future.

The first game had apparently sold terribly, but something about watching his daughter pour even more passion into the second one must have stirred Ols.

He invited Doguru to research and develop space-rated work suits.

A suit that leveraged etheric bio-prosthetics technology to support the body's movements without impeding them, suited for weightless maneuvers and precision work.

If that gained recognition and public acceptance, it would make it easier to introduce full-body etheric replacement prostheses to the world in time — and gradually, the groundwork for accepting beings like Sena would form.

In the modern era, surveillance networks for security purposes existed everywhere.

Sena appeared entirely human at first glance, but through the mechanical eyes of cameras that analyzed their subjects, she was an anomalous presence.

Virtually no one had a fully prosthetic body, and wherever she went, she stood out.

Even without malice, simply going about her daily life meant being singled out.

*To hide a tree, use a forest* — that had been his thinking all along.

Ols's plan was a roundabout one that would take an enormous span of years, but measured against the enormous span of years Sena would live, it was not a bad one.

Whether the seeds they planted would sprout as hoped, he couldn't say — but if such a future were possible in a hundred, two hundred — a thousand years, it was a dream worth dreaming.

Doguru accepted the proposal, took his friend's hand again, and returned to the path of research.

——Looking back, it had been a life that never grew dull.

"Doctor Chestnut-Head, your tea is ready."

"……Ah, thank you."

He sat up in bed, scratched his head, and reached toward the side table.

Short, white hair — he had long since gotten used to it.

Every time Doguru's hair had grown back since then, Sena would ask, *Shall I trim your hair?* — and thanks to her, he had spent his life with a slightly-longer-than-shaved, somewhat awkward hairstyle.

She must have been terribly fond of the name Chestnut-Head.

The first time he ever saw what could be called a sorrowful expression on her face was when Doguru mentioned wanting to let his hair grow out a little.

*"……S-Sena?"*

*"……No. If you say my grooming is unnecessary, then it is nothing."*

*"Th-that's not what I meant——"*

After that, Doguru never again commented on how she maintained his hair.

Sena sat in the bedside chair with her usual expressionless face, eating a cookie and watching Doguru.

"Your condition has worsened. Are you still unwilling to have your body replaced with a prosthetic frame?"

"The times haven't caught up yet. And even if I extended my life, there are still technical limits to the brain's lifespan…… I wouldn't be able to watch over you forever."

Fifty years had passed in a flash.

Development had gone well; they had further developed a skin suit that allowed even humans who could not use ether to employ virtual musculature, and the latest model was beginning to hit the market.

*Ah, I've done everything I could do,* he thought — and then he collapsed.

Etheric abnormality — what might be called the natural lifespan of a long-lived species.

Even in the modern era, where so much had been elucidated, ether remained full of unknowns.

The standard palliative treatment was brain transplant.

The fundamental issues were immune deficiency and organ failure from loss of etheric protection.

A brain transplant into a bio-cultivated body would save him, and with the etheric prosthetic bodies he and Sena had developed, he could even obtain a superior form.

Many who faced this chose to prolong their lives that way, but Doguru's life thus far had been half-afterlife to begin with.

Having felt, after all that, that he had done what he could — well, letting nature take its course seemed right.

"……According to one theory, though."

"……?"

"There's something called a soul, which we can't perceive — and the body is its vessel. It's said that this soul is what operates ether."

Among ether scholars, there were more than a few who supported such a soul hypothesis.

Even after unraveling the brain, which was supposed to govern thought, humanity had yet to determine what influenced ether.

Studying brain activity during ether manipulation had revealed tendencies, but how it directly affected ether remained unknown.

"It hasn't been scientifically proven — more like a matter of faith."

"……Faith."

*I was half-skeptical myself,* he said with a laugh.

"For long-lived species like us, the timing at which we lose the ability to operate ether often coincides with finishing something significant, or finding satisfaction…… Well, it happens when the soul has finished its business in this world, or so the theory goes. Having experienced it firsthand, I can see the appeal."

"Do you mean you want to die?"

"Not in a negative sense — more that I feel at ease."

He smiled wryly at her characteristically blunt phrasing and brought the tea to his lips.

He was still healthy enough to drink her tea on his own.

The game development she had been doing with Miri and the others had gained considerable attention with the second title, and the three of them had founded a company.

There had been some issues along the way, but it eventually became a subsidiary of Roktarna, and now it occupied a niche in the conglomerate's entertainment division.

Miri and Saria had apparently been laying groundwork behind the scenes to install Sena as the next president, and the news that "an AI may become CEO" had made headlines not long ago.

There had been some controversy, of course, but given the nature of the business — providing entertainment to the public — and thanks to the groundwork laid by Ols and the two women, the majority of media coverage was favorable, and no major — no violent — incidents occurred.

In the process, Doguru's undignified nickname of "Doctor Chestnut-Head" had become known to the world through an interview, but if her deadpan yet disarming personality had helped alleviate the fear of "an AI that might rebel against humanity," it wasn't such a bad thing.

In any case, her future was not shrouded in darkness, but illuminated by bright light.

"You've been accepted by society. There will be plenty of problems ahead, but you'll be able to overcome them. On top of that, my research has reached a stopping point…… Thinking that a good future awaits you going forward — the tension just left me."

Doguru said this, and laughed.

"My soul, if that's what it is, must have decided that this was enough."

"……It is difficult to understand."

"Fufu, it is difficult, isn't it."

The same expressionless face as always.

And yet, one that looked — just a little — lonely.

"Will you miss me when I'm gone?"

"I have not finished repaying you, so it would cause me difficulty."

*I see,* Doguru said with a wry smile, and stroked her head.

"If I were to give you a hint about that old question of yours, then, let me think."

Sena tilted her head, and Doguru spoke.

"Alberinea probably had a great many repayments of her own. And making those repayments was the most important thing in the world to her."

"A great many repayments……?"

"Yes. More than she could ever finish repaying."

When he stroked her head, she narrowed her eyes, and her lips softened just a little.

With this much, Sena had surely stockpiled a great deal of "repayment."

That was how she would go on — endlessly stockpiling an intangible something she called "repayment."

Repaying and repaying and never feeling that the debt was settled — a something that bore the name "repayment."

Why had Alberinea not given it a different name? he wondered.

There was a name far more fitting than any other.

If she had, then Sena would not be agonizing like this; the question would have dissolved and vanished in an instant.

Acting out her own story on the stage of Alberan, she must have seen it with her own eyes countless times.

And still she hadn't noticed, still she struggled.

"It's something like the nickname you gave me."

"……?"

"I think Alberinea left that as homework for you — to see what beautiful name you would give it. Just a feeling, though."

Alberinea must have treasured that feeling above all else.

It was not something for someone else to glibly teach — it was something to be decided for oneself.

"In any case, if Alberinea saw you now, I'm sure she'd be overjoyed."

"Is that so?"

"Yes. Because she must have wanted you to struggle earnestly like this."

Doguru closed his eyes and nodded.

"She is someone who passed on long, long ago…… but watching you, even I can properly understand what she wished for when she created you."

Granted an abundance of knowledge, yet knowing nothing, like a newborn.

Given only the necessary materials, a bewildered and innocent child.

It was precisely because she had wanted to cherish the process — the process of that child finding the answer on her own — that Sena was so full of contradictions.

Alberinea must have been the kind of person who valued irrational things above all.

That much, at least, Doguru understood — and with that quiet certainty, he smiled and brought the tea to his lips.

"Um, Doctor Chestnut-Head."

He turned toward the voice, and —

"I believe Master is still alive."

——He spit out his tea.

# Chapter 362: In a Far, Far Future — End

"This victory is truly thanks to you — to the Red Goddesses. The rule of those savage sorcerers is at an end…… A new era dawns. Captain Elisia, name your reward. You are a hero."

A victory over the wicked great empire of Kleinmeil, which had ruled the world for a thousand years.

In its imperial capital, Albernaria, a glittering banquet celebrating the triumph was underway.

Invited among the guests was Elisia — a beauty in military dress with red hair pulled back — captain of the "Red Goddesses."

"Swift restoration of public order. ……The indiscriminate mage-hunts being conducted everywhere are nothing but slaughter. With us waving the banner of righteousness while oppressing the weak, what justice do we truly possess?"

Cold water dashed on the reveling men of power — the sort of thing that draws scowls.

The room fell silent, and that silence was strangely satisfying.

She hadn't expected her words to change anything.

To the very end, the Red Goddesses suffered not a single death.

Alone, that one unit killed every last thing that threatened red hair.

Without its fatal weakness, the empire would have lasted another thousand years.

A mana crystal of the first Alberinea, incomprehensible to anyone in the world.

The tyranny of Kleinmeil was why her brother had starved to death.

And it did.

"We are nothing like you. Not as pure."

Both sides were simply protecting what mattered to them.

As a substitute for the pretense of justice, that was sufficient — and fighting on that basis through the years, she rose through the ranks and came to lead three hundred soldiers.

A red-horned Jaleia Gashea.

Firepower superiority through magical weaponry, iron war-golems that knew no fear of death — the coalition, despite consistently outnumbering them several times over, saw lives melt away like butter on a hot plate.

Deranged instruments of slaughter, created by Alberinea's own hand.

A slab-like greatsword in its right hand, a mana projector in its left.

Forgetting the gentle prayer from a thousand years ago, sealed within this mana crystal.

Set the direction, resolve the development team's issues, handle negotiations, and spend the days.

Analyze the information circulating in the world, buy low, add value, and sell high for profit.

The other screamed, wings thrashing — then another boom.

A ri away, two colossal bodies crashed to earth with twin tremors.

Before the astonished eyes of Lilybel's party, solemn fanfare rang out.

"Ooh……something's playing."

Above Krische's head, light heralded a level-up.

Luminous feathers scattered, and the fanfare sounded again.

A dazzling storm of luminous feathers celebrated Krische, who had achieved three consecutive level-ups in an instant.

Above the hero's head too, the same fanfare and luminous feathers appeared.

"Ah, I see……so that's how the system works. Perhaps the world itself has some sort of magic cast over it……"

The hero's level had risen to 328.

Krische's level was, as expected, still undisplayed.

*It's probably some mistake — the hero is most likely Krische-sama.*

That was what was explained to them as the dragon — called a "flying lizard" over there — was being butchered.

The level was so high it simply could not be displayed.

That must have been what it was.

'The Gentle Hunter (Mythic)' meant exactly that — a gentle hunter.

A figure that robbed her quarry of life in an instant, without suffering — the dragon's skull had burst apart, and indeed, there would not have been time to feel any pain.

When told she was a gentle hunter, the scene forced a reluctant *I suppose so* out of the observer.

Over there, these were game animals that were eaten from time to time, and the butchering was practiced and efficient.

*The flying lizards here don't have scales, so this is easy*, she remarked as the skin was stripped away in no time, leaving nothing but meat.

Directed by the two, the escort guards' swords were put to use as butchering knives.

The leftover meat was taken through the doorway to a storehouse on the other side, and the journey resumed.

The next day, they stopped at a village where the residents begged them to deal with a monster ravaging the mountain, and they encountered a boar-type monster worthy of being called the mountain's lord, but——

"Ah, right around here, just as expected."

She punched cleanly through its armored hide and pierced its heart with a strike too fast to see.

Even foes that would require a whole army were merely game to be hunted.

As a result of the dragons expanding their territory, every village and town was suffering from monster attacks — but one after another, they were skewered by her spear, had their throats slit by curved sword, or had their heads pulverized by a thrown lance, all becoming that evening's dinner.

Without alerting the monsters — which possessed the keen senses of wild beasts — Krische dispatched them effortlessly.

The estate's pet, Gururun, playfully swatted at the head of a monster that would have made everyone else tense up, and snapped its neck with casual brutality.

The Royal Guard Captain Yuris, who couldn't even begin to compete — the maid Bery watched it all with a warm smile, clapping.

The phrase *a world apart* was perhaps an understatement.

The monsters feared in this world were, to them, probably something like rabbits.

The rate of leveling up was also from another world entirely.

Every time Krische hunted a new monster, every time she butchered one, her level would jump by several ranks, and fanfare would ring out.

Above the head of Gururun, who was just playing, fanfare.

Above the head of Bery, who did nothing but watch with a smile, somehow, occasionally, fanfare.

Perhaps the goddess's fanfare had a sale season too.

Level represented strength.

Through experience, as magical power and technique grew, one's rank rose one step at a time.

Some spent ten or twenty years climbing from Level 10 to Level 11, while others went from 40 to 50 in the same span.

Innate talent was a cruel thing.

Even doing the same things, the experience gained differed vastly.

Swinging a sword for the same amount of time, or slaying the same monster, did not yield equal returns.

The one thing that was certain: the higher you climbed, the more experience was needed — that single truth.

After decades of training to reach a certain level, raising it by just one more required tremendous effort.

Enough that people would hold feasts to celebrate a single level-up.

Level was something you built up.

The higher you built, the harder it naturally became to gain new experience.

What on earth were these people gaining experience from?

Every time the fanfare sounded, Lilybel's party could only fall silent.

Another dragon appeared — cleanly pierced through the heart in a single throw. From a ri away.

This time it had apparently been deliberate; the spear, which had shattered the dragon's head the other day, remained embedded and intact.

That she had even accounted for the falling dragon's trajectory was evident from——

'*Hmm, if I take it down like this, the spear can be reused.*'

'*Fufu, you're very skilled, Krische-sama.*'

'*Ehehe, they have a habit of rearing up when they roar, so if you time it to that, you could do it too, Bery. They fall on their backs, so it works out.*'

——the pair's satisfied inspection of the spear.

A maid and a princess who leveled up even while butchering game.

Perhaps they were gaining enormous experience from the monsters' anatomical structure — which spots a spear would kill most efficiently, which points were most vulnerable.

Yuris, looking deeply contemplative, seemed to interpret it that way.

*By all means, if it is not too much trouble, I would like to learn swordsmanship from you*, she said, bowing her head — and what the two told her was that their approach to combat and their use of power were fundamentally different. The starting distance of engagement was five or six ken.

Even with a spear, a distance where nothing would come close to landing — and unlike this world, which prioritized power, their side prioritized speed.

Maximizing the use of one's entire body, combined with divinely precise and fluid magical power management, produced extraordinary lunges and strikes.

The effective killing range itself was vastly different; what appeared to be a bizarrely wide gap was in practice no different from having a sword swung right before your face.

A single motion of the sword — that was all it took for a lunge that defied common sense.

Only when one could do that did Krische say it met the *minimum*.

That was, of course, the perspective of someone at Krische's transcendent level.

In reality, even in their world, not many could combine a four- or five-ken lunge with a single sword stroke, Bery said — but in terms of speed, their swordsmanship doubtlessly overwhelmed what existed here.

The combat arts of their world, they said, had always been developed with human opponents in mind.

Here, by contrast, Bery suggested that the combat arts had evolved to prioritize power, aimed at defeating monsters.

This world teemed with monsters that threatened people, and many possessed tough hides or shells; soldiers all trained to fight such creatures.

In their world, however, magical beasts were rare, and seldom appeared on the highways.

Outside a few specific regions, dedicated training for magical beast extermination simply didn't exist — that was how uncommon they were.

When told this was a product of differing objectives, one could nod in understanding.

And then there was the difference in magical power.

Compared to their world, the people here apparently possessed far more internal magical power; in their world, magic was performed using ambient magical power from the surroundings.

Using only one's internal magical power for magic was extremely difficult.

If one could use a magic sword to cleave several ken ahead, the result was not much different from their method of bringing those ken within killing range through sheer footwork.

It might be better to refine that approach, Bery said — though at the earnest request of Yuris and the others, she somewhat reluctantly instructed them.

Yuris and the hand-picked guards were all treated like children.

The words "just a refinement" — what sort of modesty had that been?

A single swing revealed the ultimate ideal.

What the master and servant demonstrated was the pinnacle of swordsmanship.

Press your strengths against the opponent's weaknesses.

Prevent the opponent from taking strong actions — use disruption and feints to constrain or neutralize their movements, inducing weaker responses.

Ultimately it was a building-block puzzle, they said — words that could only come from those who had reached the summit.

Once you arrived at your own highest potential, all that remained was figuring out how to handle the opponent, and the ringing fanfare was precisely that.

Meeting a new monster, learning its habits and anatomy, discovering its weaknesses — all of that directly translated to strength.

That the hero leveled up from building-block puzzles was what it meant to be a hero.

The two had already reached a realm where they no longer even needed to swing a sword — comprehensible in theory, yet impossible to accept.

King of kings and ruler equal to the gods, Kreschenta Farna Vera Alberan (self-proclaimed).

*Why* must you win?

A level that had stalled for three years had jumped by three, and she now stood at 60.

"The power they possess, which surpasses all our understanding, may have been cultivated precisely because they lived in a world enveloped by Senkii."

To their words, Yaakuai——

"Just so. ……To believe is also a teaching of the Goddess Yurin."

——narrowed his already narrow eyes further and nodded gently.

Then — one more week later — at dusk on the accursed Mount Chiu-Neen, at the summit.

In a makeshift cooking station set up with charcoal grills and stock pots, the three watched the girl Krische, her expression more serious than they had ever seen.

At a glance, the movements seemed flowing and flawless — but to a discerning eye, the hesitation was visible.

Regular yet irregular, subtle yet bold.

As if painting a picture, she layered gradations of flavor.

'*Well then — as a memento to mark the end of the journey, may I ask Krische-sama to handle the main cutlet?*'

The moment she heard those casual words from the hero, the girl who had been so cheerful carved deep furrows between her brows.

First, she sampled thin slices from each cut as if appraising them, and selected the base of the tail — the most flavorful part of the dragon.

Precisely calculated gradations of flavor.

What she aimed for was an evolving depth of taste with each small bite Bery took.

Beside her sister, infected by the intense atmosphere, Kreschenta held out the silver tray with a trace of tension.

The monarch of all dragons.

Swallowing the dragon flesh, it swept its tail with an irritated air.

A shrill, whistle-like sound rang across the land, and in response, dragon cries — like screams — echoed from all around.

The Whistle of Calamity, recorded in legend.

——————————————

- Class: Evil Dragon
- Level: 255
- Unique Ability: He Who Commands the Dragons (Exceptional)

Level 255 — heights as staggering as the hero's.

A realm attainable only by stacking formulas of absolute precision in staggering numbers.

Whether one could reach Level 255 purely through that was questionable.

That enormous body, that crushing presence.

Atop the mighty magical beast called Gururun.

Had she not been listening?

Full combat posture — it had marked them as enemies.

A cry that shook heaven and earth, the evil dragon of calamity — her innards were being squeezed—— or would have been, but it stopped almost immediately.

Not a single significant chip — it was uncannily beautiful.

The signs of extreme use were obvious at a glance, and yet it didn't look as though it had been used as a weapon on the battlefield.

"……'Extraordinary' doesn't even begin to cover it. She charged into the enemy practically alone, and this is what it looks like."

Selene sighed, looking exasperated.

Then she gazed at the sword, sadness in her eyes.

"Please do what you can to restore it. She was worried — said she felt bad because she'd been a little rough with it."

Koz nodded — *As you wish* — and Selene left with a *please*.

For a while, his father studied Krische Christand's sword.

"This is incredible……"

"It's like a kitchen knife. Not a sword that's been through combat."

Then he handed the sword to Keiz.

"What do you think? Doesn't it make you want to forge a sword for someone who wields a blade like that? A sword into which you've poured every last ounce of your body and soul — your life's work."

"Well……"

Keiz began, then bit back the words.

"I have regrets. It was only meant for self-defense……that was the mindset I brought to it. The sword itself isn't bad, to be sure — but it was inadequate for Krische-sama's skill. Had I tempered it harder, this would not have happened — there wouldn't even be edge-dulling."

Indeed, that was true.

The edge had not chipped — it had been crushed.

The result of a blade swung with flawless precision.

"I unconsciously compromised because it was merely a self-defense sword. Against a wielder of this caliber, I failed to give my best. This dulling is not Krische-sama's doing — it is mine. ……As a smith, there is nothing more shameful."

"Surely that's going too far……you only spoke with her briefly, and to expect you to know——"

"Of course. There's no way I could have known. But……suppose one day, someone walks in off the street and places an order — can you tell how skilled a swordsman they are?"

Keiz frowned and shook his head.

"You can't know their ability, their talent, anything. Yet the person who walks in today might be a swordsman beyond all imagination — or might become one someday. ……That is precisely why you must always give your best, and deliver the finest sword you can. That is the mindset that matters."

Koz looked at Keiz.

"I'm not talking to you about business. I'm talking about a way of life."

"……A way of life."

"You're fully capable. You could make a living anywhere. If you genuinely want to pursue the commercial path — if you truly mean to master the business side — I won't object. But don't live on inertia."

With that, Koz drew Selene's longsword from its scabbard.

A slender yet thick, sturdy longsword.

Bearing countless scratches, yet only a single notable chip.

"……It is neither age nor ability. A person who defines their own way of living and walks straight ahead — such a person is as beautiful as fine-tempered steel. And when such a person walks a harsh road, we offer prayers that our work may support them. ……That is the nature of this craft."

He returned the sword to its scabbard and looked at Keiz again.

"What we forge is not steel. Through the steel, through the hammer, we hammer out our own souls. With each swing, we ask — *is this enough?* — and we keep striking until the weakness is gone……until it is sublimated into something beautiful."

A straight, unwavering gaze — strong as a warrior's.

"There is always something to be gained on a hard, painful, difficult road. ……Just once. Try forging a single blade without compromise."

He stood and placed a hand on Keiz's shoulder.

"Whether this path is worth spending your one and only life on — you'll know for yourself after that. As a father, I want you to live a life rich in meaning."

*He's an artist*, Keiz thought.

Different from himself, after all — that thought came again.

He understood, to a degree, what his father was saying.

He had watched his father's work since the day he was born.

Not for money, not for fame — his father was the most honest craftsman he knew.

There were times he took on jobs for what amounted to pocket change.

Occasionally, someone would come back years later to express deep gratitude and commission more work, paying in gold coins.

But it was a trade that dealt in weapons.

Sometimes those customers never returned at all; some gave up on their aspirations halfway; and more than a few sold the sword without so much as a word of thanks.

Precisely because he understood how much of himself his father poured into each blade, there was an inexpressible helplessness and frustration.

When sincerity was not met with sincerity in return, his father said nothing.

No matter what Keiz said, the answer was always the same: *A sword I've sold.*

His father's swords were beautiful.

A blade forged without compromise — it sounded simple, but it was not.

The better his technique grew, the more flaws he could see, and to leave a flaw was to compromise.

The elegant uniformity of the steel his father hammered out was Koz's very soul made manifest.

The more he saw people handle that carelessly, the more he questioned whether it was worth going that far.

Most people could not appreciate his father's work.

The painstaking attention to detail — steel shaped to fit the owner's build, strength, and purpose — they could neither see it nor understand it.

His father's steel was more valuable than the gold-coin "masterpieces" that most would call beautiful — showy things, all surface glitter.

And yet — *what is the point of going that far* — that was what he kept thinking.

Keiz knew how to sell his father's swords for far more.

His father, naturally, understood it too.

Add some fittings, dress up the scabbard, polish the steel to a cosmetic shine — wealthy buyers would come flocking.

Such offers had come many times, and that was arguably the proper value of his father's steel.

He admired the beauty of the path his father walked, yet the reason he couldn't commit was precisely this.

Unlike his father, Keiz could not simply accept *a sword I've sold* and leave it at that.

If it were going to someone who could appreciate its worth, then perhaps — but otherwise——

* * *

——A little over a year later.

After the civil war known as the War of Succession had ended, the visitors were Bery, the Christand household servant, and Krische, who had been granted the title of Heavenly Sword. And two women under her command.

"……I'm sorry. Koz-san put so much care into forging that sword for Krische……Krische had meant to treasure it for the rest of her life, but."

"Ah, no, please don't worry about it. To have been used to this extent — the sword itself would call it a good life. Blades are consumables by nature; they wear down with use and sharpening."

And indeed, that a blade should wear with use and sharpening was only natural.

Even so, the degree of wear defied imagination.

If it had been a kitchen knife passed down through generations, it might have been understandable — but this was not the kind of wear a weapon saw on the battlefield.

The civil war had lasted perhaps half a year. A blade would break before it wore down like this.

That it still held its shape as a sword was a miracle.

It was clear that the wielder had treated the thinned blade with utmost care — parting flesh as if using a kitchen knife, avoiding bone, never clashing edge against edge.

"To begin with, it was a touch too slender for battlefield use. The failure was mine."

What came from Koz — as when he had last sharpened the blade — were words spoken from the heart.

If it had been broken through misuse, that would have been one thing.

It had been forged strong enough not to break under proper handling, of course, but there were limits.

And yet — abnormal though the skill was — Koz's sword had clearly failed to keep pace with the wielder's ability.

That was something his father could never forgive.

From that day, Koz swung his hammer as if he'd forgotten to eat or sleep.

So lost in his work that no one dared speak to him.

Since the last time they'd seen it, he had been experimentally forging the same type of curved sword — testing ore from various regions, examining the balance of impurities.

Heating the iron mass in the furnace and hammering it to refine it, driving out impurities.

Pursuing ever-greater purity — yet simple purity alone would not do.

The balance of the impurities that remained was what determined the steel's worth.

Neither too soft nor too hard.

That exquisite balance was what made a sword a sword.

Soft steel resisted chipping and was durable, but the edge would flatten easily, and it would wear down in no time.

The previous curved sword had been that sort of blade.

Conversely, steel that was too hard would chip and snap.

The notion that one needed only to make a hard edge was an amateur's thinking.

An edge that would not wear from cutting flesh, that would not crush from cleaving bone, yet possessed enough resilience never to crack — it was like balancing a spear point on another spear point.

A balance bordering on the miraculous was required.

And red-hot steel did not announce its own condition.

He judged by the faintest change in hue, the pattern of sparks, the feel transmitted through the hammer, and the ring of the sound.

So wholly was his father's mind directed at the steel that calling out to him went unheard.

Within the shadow play, sparks scattered.

Like something no longer human, he swung the hammer in a trance of single-minded focus.

*——Lost in that madness, and yet, my father is beautiful.*

"How's it going, Keiz? How's Koz doing?"

"……Same as you hear — morning to night, same as always."

He was checking the charcoal stock out back when someone called to him — Fasero, the gray-haired master of the leatherworks, his hair pulled back in a knot.

They outsourced some leather goods — scabbards, buckles — to his workshop.

"Your grandpa was a dedicated man, but your old man takes it even further. And with it being a sword for Krische-sama, who's become the Queen's blade — all the more so."

"Even so, he's pushing himself too hard. He's not young anymore. Mother and I are worried he's going to ruin his health."

"Can't be helped. That's what it means to be a craftsman."

Fasero laughed.

"Sure, there are guys who work for pocket change like they're shopkeepers. But you can't call that craftsmanship — a real craftsman devotes everything to their art."

"I've heard that from the old man so many times I've got calluses on my ears."

"You keep getting told because you haven't taken it to heart."

Saying this, Fasero sat on a nearby barrel.

"The clothes you're wearing, the barrel I'm sitting on, your house — you don't find it all impressive?"

"……?"

"How many people do you think had a hand in making all that?"

"Well……"

When Keiz couldn't answer and trailed off, Fasero continued.

"Thousands, tens of thousands of years — all sorts of craftsmen dedicated their lives to the skills that support us now. From the big things to the small, right down to the humblest little tool — someone had to rack their brains to make it."

He gazed skyward with distant eyes and smiled.

"Because of those people, we can eat, we can live. So we do our work with respect for those who came before us. ……Shoddy work would be smearing mud on their faces."

*In your case, it might be harder to see*, he said, looking at Keiz.

"For me, it was my master — the reason's close at hand. I fell in love with my master's work, and that's how I came to this trade. But for you, it's your own father……you don't fully appreciate how lucky you are to see first-class work up close every day. That said — your old man's pretty cool, wouldn't you say?"

"……Well, of course I respect him."

He respected his father more than anyone. He believed him to be a true craftsman.

But whether he wanted to become like him — that was a different question.

There were times he couldn't help thinking: *You don't have to go that far.*

"Your grandpa died young. Koz came back from the war with that injury, and a few years later the old man passed. ……Your old man regrets not having been able to properly inherit all of his father's craft."

*A human life is short*, Fasero said.

"To take the work our predecessors built and move it one step forward — that's how you repay them. And that alone takes extraordinary effort. But your old man is probably swinging that hammer for his father too — the father who fell halfway along the road. And for him, right now is probably that moment."

"That moment……?"

"The moment of repaying that debt. *My entire life existed for this one blade* — I'm certain that's what he's thinking. And he wants you to see it."

Fasero stood and clapped Keiz firmly on the shoulder.

Then said:

"If you've got time to stand around here shooting the breeze, go watch your old man work. This is the job of a lifetime — everything he's ever been is going into it."

"……Yes."

"He's too awkward with words to say it himself, but that blade is the one he wants *you* to see most of all. ……Stop thinking pointless thoughts and go watch, properly."

He ruffled Keiz's hair and turned on his heel.

"Tell Koz I'm waiting for the greatest sword he's ever made. It's a sword for Krische-sama of House Christand, after all. Everyone here will answer with their best work too."

That was the catalyst.

After that, Keiz too began spending his time in the forge, where before he hadn't lingered for fear of getting in the way.

His mother laughed wryly, telling him not to worry about the shop, and his father said nothing — simply accepted Keiz's presence when he tried to help.

Hardness at the cutting edge, yet without overall excess rigidity.

What his father struggled with was the refining stage before the forging proper. The goal was not perfect uniformity, but a gradient of hardness — a balance.

Hard at the edge, gradually softening toward the spine.

An edge hard enough not to crush, cushioned by a body supple enough to absorb the shock.

And through it all, not a single flaw or irregularity in the grain.

Compounding the difficulty was the shape — not a straight blade but a curve.

The arc that bent forward in a く-shape had to be formed without disrupting the balance.

Painstaking refining might, with luck, produce a suitable base — and upon that, extraordinary technique could, with luck, yield a finished blade.

That process of forging had already surpassed the domain of human ability.

The beauty of his father, hammering in a trance of total unity with the tool, was found precisely there.

To create what a human could not create, he cast off even his humanity.

His shadow play told Keiz: *I exist for this and nothing else.*

The sparks that flew were impurities — they were Koz the smith, the human being himself.

But even so, there were limits.

His father, lost in his work to the exclusion of food and sleep, was now in his twilight years——

"——Old man!"

One day, he collapsed as if consciousness had simply cut out.

"Just exhaustion, he says. ……Honestly, this man."

"……Sorry. I should have forced him to rest more."

His mother, whose face had gone ashen, finally relaxed at the doctor's verdict, and stroked Koz's forehead as he slept.

Her expression was troubled — the look she might give a child.

"He wouldn't have listened even if you'd told him. I'm glad he's been forced to rest."

His mother smiled wryly, and Keiz asked.

"What do you think, Mom?"

"Think about what?"

"The old man's work. You never try to stop him."

His mother shook the hair that was showing more white now, thought for a moment, and smiled softly.

"I married into this family because I loved your father's work. The technical side is completely lost on me, but——"

She said it sheepishly, gazing at Koz.

"——I do think your father's work is very beautiful. Nothing but pure effort, and honesty……I fell in love with that way of living. Being able to watch it up close, and support it — that's my happiness."

*There's no 'what do you think' about it*, she said with a rueful laugh.

"It was Krische-sama — now the kingdom's greatest hero — who came back and asked your father specifically for a commission. Someone like that recognized his work, truly understood it……you can't imagine how happy that makes him. He won't grumble the way you do, but he's been through so much hardship and heartache."

Keiz couldn't find words, and his mother chuckled.

"……That makes me happy too. I could never stop him. Completing this work perfectly — that is your father's greatest happiness, I'm sure."

"Mom……"

"Oh, no — that's not quite right."

His mother gave Keiz a meaningful look.

"Your father's *greatest* happiness is having his beloved apprentice watch that perfect work."

Then she asked him: *Don't you have better things to do than standing around here?*

And so, Keiz swung the hammer in the forge.

He couldn't match his father's work, but he could assist.

He loaded high-quality iron ore, sourced from Arna, into the furnace, and hammered the red-hot mass to drive out impurities.

It required not just technique, but a measure of luck.

The ideal was to expel only the unwanted impurities and retain what was needed.

Whether any given ore was a winner couldn't be known until it was worked. But given perfect material, his father would do perfect work.

Resilience on one side, hardness on the other.

He hammered the ore into a sheet, folded it, declared *this isn't it*, and tossed it aside.

What he sought was not his own ideal but his father's. With every strike, his father's scowling face surfaced in his mind.

It was a strange sensation.

He brought the hammer down; sparks flew. The red-hot mass glowed before him.

That was all the world contained.

The furnace lit the darkness, and the sparks danced and scattered.

He lost track of time; his arms, swinging the hammer, seemed tireless.

Each stroke fell like a question posed to the iron, and only the sound and sparks answered.

*Good enough* and *high quality* — those words had no place here.

*Genuine* or *reject* — only that.

*Worthy of his father's hammer, or not* — only that.

From waking to sleeping, every hour given to the forge.

Even mealtimes felt like intrusions, so deeply had he entered the shadow-play world.

When he found it, he knew by the sound.

The sparks, everything — behaving exactly as he'd hoped.

A shiver crawled up his spine — something like a chill.

*This is what I was looking for* — and with that understanding, he struck again and again, filling the air with sparks and sound.

Then, having set the hammer down and savored the afterglow——

"Want to finish it yourself?"

"——! Old man……you were watching?"

At the entrance to the forge — Koz stood with arms crossed, back against the wall.

"……Don't be ridiculous. From here on it's your job. If this were a straight blade, maybe — but finishing this is beyond me right now."

Keiz stood, and for an instant, the world tilted.

Koz caught him at once.

"I'm done. You've rested enough, haven't you? Finish your own work."

"……I see."

"You'd better not have any complaints about this one."

He said it as he sank into the other chair and drank water.

Koz took his place before the anvil and studied the drawn-out steel for a time.

Silent, still, eyes narrowed — then he picked up the hammer.

"……Do you like smithing, Keiz?"

"It's hot and exhausting. A rotten job."

"I see."

Koz gave a rueful chuckle and said:

"I feel the same. I took over from my old man because it was the family trade, but I've wished more than a few times it was something easier."

*I prefer watching*, he added, and Keiz laughed — *Me too.*

"Someday, show your own child."

And with that, his father became a shadow play, and brought the hammer down.

A spark bloomed, and a note rang out.

Rhythmically — light blossoming like flowers — small flashes winked on and off.

Just as Keiz had, his father too must have grown up watching shadow plays like this.

And someday, like his father, Keiz too would become a shadow play.

Picturing his father's hammer in his mind's eye, scattering sparks, dissolving into the scenery.

"……Still — it's not a bad job."

When Keiz said it, the shadow play paused its hammer for just an instant.

And answered with sparks, laughing.

* * *

Past the row of brick workshops lining the cluttered street — on the signboard of that smithy hung the simplified crest of Christand.

A boy who'd been caught heading out to play and ordered by his mother to mind the shop lay slumped face-down on the counter, looking thoroughly fed up.

The door opened, and he raised his head to find a girl with silver hair.

She wore a black cloak, and beneath it, a rather fine dress.

Three or four years older than him, maybe — a staggeringly beautiful girl, but he couldn't fathom why she'd visit a smithy like this. He tilted his head, wondering what she wanted.

"……? Welcome."

"Hello. Is Keiz-san here?"

"He's here, but……Gramps is working with my old man right now."

He jerked his chin toward the back.

The sound of hammering had been going on without pause.

"What's the business?"

"Well, you see, Krische would like to have a sword forged as a gift."

As she spoke, she removed the belt at her waist, and two swords were placed before the boy.

He frowned.

They were those oddly-shaped barbarian swords that the shop stocked.

He'd been told that the hero called Alberinea used them, and they sold reasonably well as self-defense weapons.

One was well-maintained but clearly old; the other, from scabbard onward, was an elaborately crafted piece.

He was surprised to see two, but the strange combination was what puzzled him most.

"It's for one of the students at the academy. She says she'd like a curved sword similar to this one, for self-defense."

"We don't make ornamental swords here. We turn that kind of work down, no matter the money."

He indicated the finely wrought curved sword and told her. The girl nodded and pointed to the other one.

"Ah, yes. Something simple like this one will be fine. Your grandson?"

"Yep. Name's Koz."

"Koz……"

"Named after my great-grandpa. He's the guy who forged swords for Alberinea — a really incredible smith. If you use this kind of sword, you must've heard of him, right?"

"Yes, of course……"

His great-grandfather was famous for having forged swords for Alberinea.

Being given that name was the boy's secret pride — but his greatest frustration was that neither his father nor grandfather would let him help with the forge work.

Lately, that resentment had him sulking and playing outside all day.

"But neither of 'em will teach me a thing about the work. All I get is shop duty, and I'm sick of it. They're working right now so it'll take a bit — why don't you check out those shelves over there? We've got similar swords. Lately they've been busy, so I dunno if they'll even take a custom order."

"I see……"

"Well, the stuff on those shelves is top quality too, so no worries."

With that, the boy headed to the back.

He realized he'd forgotten to ask her name but figured, *oh well*, and went into the forge.

Opening the door, the first thing he always felt was the wave of heat.

In the dim room, only the furnace blazed, and the shadow play brought the hammer down.

*Clang* — and sparks bloomed and danced.

His grandfather's hands wielded the hammer; watching, still as stone, was the shadow play of his father.

At times like this, even calling out earned nothing more than *wait a moment*.

The work looked bold but was delicate, and if he didn't time it right — catching a natural pause — he'd be scolded.

But waiting didn't bother him.

The shadow play and the sparks — that peculiar world — watching it felt like stumbling into another realm.

The red-hot steel was everything in that space, and with each hammer blow, silence seemed to spread.

As if every other sound in the world had been erased — it was that quiet.

Anyone could tell: *this must not be interrupted.*

The hammer's trajectory, landing with unerring precision every time — was indescribably beautiful.

"A customer? Koz."

After a while, it was his father who asked.

He didn't look their way; his eyes were on the glowing steel.

"Oh, yeah. Some fancy-looking weird lady's here. A commission for Gramps."

"Name?"

"Forgot to ask, but she's got silver hair and she's crazy pretty. She wants a sword forged — one of those Alberinea swords — as a present."

At those words, the shadow play's hammer stopped dead.

After a time, the figure rose, and rough, calloused hands settled on the boy's head.

"Koz — for the next commission, from start to finish, you're going to be in the forge watching. Understood?"

"……Me too?"

"Yes. ……It seems my turn has come."

The shadow play gazed at the hammer in its hands, smiled, and said so.

# Chapter 365: Alberan I

It should have been a battle they could never lose.

That tribe was a people whose only merit was their dexterity with their hands.

Flatterers who survived by fawning and prostrating themselves before other tribes — spineless weaklings without a shred of pride.

Enreine — the people of the shadows, just as the contemptuous name once given to them declared.

Before the great tribe Aldos, led by Bernaik, they should never have been permitted to even raise their heads.

And yet, their conduct over this past year had been beyond intolerable.

Whatever had possessed them to such arrogance, they had gone so far as to utter the madness of refusing to supply weapons without payment in gold, had cut down his envoy, and sent the head back lashed to a horse.

It was an insult sufficient to warrant the annihilation of their entire clan.

That such a thing had occurred under Bernaik's reign as great chieftain of Aldos was a stain that would endure for generations.

It had smeared mud across the radiant future Bernaik had envisioned — to make Aldos the greatest tribe in all of history, in his own generation.

His elegant golden hair glimmering in the dappled light filtering through the trees, Bernaik led his valiant warriors toward the lands of Enreine.

The battlefield was a somewhat open space within the forest.

The craven of Enreine who had foolishly shown themselves numbered a hundred at most — clutching ungainly, excessively long spears, standing in orderly lines as though huddled together.

*——They can't fight properly, so they mean to win by the length of their weapons.*

Seeing this, everyone laughed, and Bernaik was no different.

Deciding there was no need for him to step in, he yielded the glory to his warriors and remained on horseback in the rear.

As he watched the three hundred warriors surge forward at his command, something felt wrong.

It would immediately become a melee, and a massacre — that was what he had expected.

The reality was the exact opposite. The Enreine men bearing their long spears, the moment Bernaik's warriors closed to near range, lowered their spears in unison at the sound of a horn, presenting their points toward the attackers without a single gap.

The warriors who had charged in as a group could not kill their momentum, and impaled upon that wall of spears, unified without the slightest break in discipline, they screamed in agony.

In an instant, dozens of skilled fighters who had been at the very front were dead, and those behind them met the same fate.

When those who had faltered heard the horn sound again, the Enreine men began to march forward in orderly formation, spears still leveled.

Their stance thrust spears both forward and overhead.

Even warriors renowned as champions could not easily leap over that to close in.

"What is this……"

The warriors were slowly pushed back, and seeing this, Bernaik bellowed.

"Fall back and regroup! Spears that long can't be swung easily. Enter the forest on both flanks and attack from multiple——"

He tried to issue orders on the spot, but the sound of bowstrings cut him off.

This was a young man he had looked after since infancy, like a grandson — rough around the edges, his mouth included, and with many flaws, but gifted and with a bright future ahead.

He prayed to the god of valor that his misgivings would not manifest in the worst possible way.

# Chapter 367: Alberan III

The battlefield was the forest of Arendol.

This area was dominated by algana trees that grew straight and branched high.

Algana produced quality timber, and ease of felling was one further advantage.

The algana's shallow, spreading root system prevented dense underbrush from growing around it — only ground cover carpeted the earth — making transportation and various other tasks convenient.

Spears, shields, bows, arrows, buildings — wood was used for everything.

A forest with quality algana was extremely valuable, and it was not uncommon for such forests to be fought over in wars.

Visibility was not too poor, but a forest was still a forest.

Scattered thickets of undergrowth obstructed the distant view, and clear sightlines could not be taken for granted.

"If they've been pushing the pace, they could appear any time now. Send word to the rear to be on guard — the last thing I want is to be bitten in the flank."

"Understood."

At the words of Godeus, clad in leather armor with a great shield strapped to his back, one of the men beside him relayed the order.

Godeus and his forces were traveling one of the paths cut through the forest — a road cleared to allow the passage of wagons.

The terrain was generally firm, but with mild undulations like small hills.

To advance the main body, this road was the only real option.

Garkha's warriors wore almost no armor, instead wielding large round shields.

The timber was algana, reinforced with iron at the rim and at the central boss cut for the fist — sturdy constructions, each painted with a colorful symbol.

Among their people, the shield was regarded as something almost more sacred than the sword, and being granted a shield was the proof of a warrior.

There was a rule that a shield engraved with one's own symbol must never be released on the battlefield — a man might be killed for the offense of stepping over another's shield.

Consequently, their skill with shields was universally high, and this was one of the reasons Garkha ruled so much territory.

Fundamentally, they did not form ranks but charged as a mass, much like most tribes — however, they coordinated with those around them on the fly, constructing improvised walls of shields.

Even when surrounded by strangers, they assessed the situation instantly, locked shields into a defensive formation, and calmly worked toward a solution — this flexibility was their weapon.

Many a tribe that had mocked their disciplined wall as the spectacle of comical turtles found itself overwhelmed by the implacable advance, then cut down the moment cracks appeared.

Battle was all about flow — especially in forest combat, where situational awareness was difficult.

Victory and defeat were often decided by the mood of the moment, and the impenetrable wall of shields was, for most warrior rabble, an invincible barrier against which they had no recourse.

When one man flinched, those who saw him flinched as well, and no matter how many formidable warriors numbered among them, once the tide had turned, the mood could not be reversed.

And their other weapon was the throwing axe.

Most Garkha warriors, in lieu of bows, carried two throwing axes on their belts — the first used at the start of battle, the second once the enemy line broke.

Kill the enemy's momentum with the first. If that was enough to crush them, well and good.

If not, weather the storm behind the shield wall, apply pressure, then shatter them completely with the second axe to claim victory.

This was the basic tactic known as 'Garkha's Two Throws.'

It was more a simple set of agreements than a proper tactic, but against the uncoordinated hordes that most opponents fielded, it gave them a decisive edge.

However, nothing was without flaws.

Their equipment was, for this era, heavy — and they were slower than lighter opponents.

Against lightly equipped enemies carrying simple armor and a single sword or bow, they lost in flexibility, and were particularly vulnerable in chance engagements.

Though the warriors carried minimum provisions, their equipment limited their capacity, and for major battles they needed wagons, restricting their movement.

When Garkha suffered major defeats, it was invariably because these weaknesses had been exploited.

Aldos was especially dangerous.

In stark contrast to Garkha, their culture held the longsword as deeply sacred.

Their ferocity — swinging two-handed swords with no fear of death — was well known, and if they were allowed to close to a melee, the battle could turn in an instant.

Berserkers who blocked thrown axes head-on with their swords.

Being drawn into a melee was the one thing that had to be avoided at all costs.

Every great tribe that ruled multiple others had its strengths.

Differences born of ancestral cultures and faiths — the tribes that had continually proven the value of theirs subjugated their neighbors and came to be called great tribes.

What had been mustered was just over two thousand.

Not a bad number, but no reason for complacency.

Fear was an invisible monster.

The instant they believed they were losing, cowardice would spread like a contagion.

Garkha prided itself on being the rock, but Aldos was a thunderstorm.

The moment they ceased to be rock, even for an instant, they would be overwhelmed.

"Great Chieftain — an Aldos force sighted ten ri ahead."

"Ha! Coming at Garkha head-on, are they? Halt the advance! Transition from column to battle formation!"

He roared at the messenger's words, donned his helmet, and moved the shield from his back to his left arm.

Two throwing axes hung at his right hip, a five-shaku short spear rode his back, and a large axe sat at his left hip.

Standard equipment for a warrior of Garkha's warrior class.

Atop his iron helm with its cheekguards rose a spike and a horsetail crest.

A well-worn piece — the helm passed down among Garkha's great chieftains.

His leather armor protected his trunk and limbs.

Shield and arrow-ward — quite a few men, trusting in their tattoo charms, fought bare-chested, but Godeus was a coolheaded realist.

It somewhat restricted movement and was inconvenient, but he understood the utility of armor.

A single arrow that happened to find a gap in the shields, a single stone — any of these could prove fatal.

In his own tenure he wanted to at minimum equip them with gauntlets and greaves, and ideally helmets.

If all the warriors donned full armor over their shields, nothing could be more reassuring.

The enemy would need to penetrate both sturdy shield and armor to inflict a lethal wound.

Warriors marching forward behind shields, fearless of falling arrows, would crush enemy formations like a rolling boulder.

If they were going to bear the weaknesses of heavy equipment, they might as well lean into it entirely — that was his philosophy. But the warriors lived in the space between reality and fantasy.

A man who fought without a single piece of protective gear, who slew many and returned alive, was indeed a hero.

The more needless risk one took, the higher the estimation as a warrior.

Their ideals of what a warrior should be — held for reasons of culture and belief — could not be so easily stripped away simply because rationality said otherwise.

What a vexing thing, he thought, eyeing the nearly half of his men who were bare-chested with tattoos, and drew a throwing axe from his hip, spinning it in a casual warm-up toss.

In fine form — if he could crush Aldos decisively, the warriors, many of whom still looked down on him, would recognize him as great chieftain.

That the enemy had come at them head-on, whatever their reasoning, was a stroke of fortune.

"Pass the word: the first throw must be in unison. From first to last, we never yield the flow — we crush them."

The great defeat at the hands of Enreine.

These two had not been present.

Bernaik, who had never bent the knee to anyone since childhood, kneeling before a slip of a girl like Bazarishe.

Bazarishe was innocent as a child.

At the root of things, she saw herself and everyone else as different creatures, tending to them the way one might tend to one's livestock.

*'I'd like you to cull the Almas and Roddia group early. Preferably in the next battle — dispose of them however you see fit.'*

Without malice, smiling — she was Alberan.

She was decidedly not a god — she was, as the word said, a child of the gods.

# Chapter 368: Alberan IV

The enemy force was to the right.

To the left, where Garkha was advancing, rose a small hill — the enemy had likely avoided the uneven terrain.

At some distance from the road — the area was notably free of undergrowth, well suited for battle.

They wheeled to face that direction.

Not lined up with the precision of a proper formation, but they had spread out and extended both wings.

Proper theory-based tactics did not yet exist, but the absence of theory did not mean the absence of tactics — these people were by no means fools.

They understood what formations were advantageous and which were not.

Extend your wings wider than the enemy's, and you could surround and fight the warriors at their flanks.

A principle anyone could grasp.

Being surrounded was worse than surrounding — anyone could see that.

The fundamentals of tactics were straightforward.

A human can only fight the opponent in front of them, so if someone else hits them from the side or behind, you win — even a child could understand such logic.

Precisely because it was logic anyone could understand, it was inevitable that everyone tried to prevent it.

Being flanked or having your back taken in battle was the mark of a fool, and a skilled warrior always maintained awareness of the whole battlefield, maneuvering to avoid exposing flanks and rear.

But their thinking stopped there.

Gather enough numbers to avoid disadvantage, and once the fighting begins, the rest is up to each individual.

In the chaos of a melee, grasping everything accurately was impossible, and even if one could issue accurate orders, they would only reach the men within ten ken.

Amid the screams and war cries — the battlefield music played at the border of life and death — there was no means of making one's voice heard accurately across all the warriors.

Even if it could be heard, whether they understood the intent was another matter, and even if the intent was perfectly understood, there was no guarantee they would follow orders.

The man receiving orders also had his own priority — killing the enemy before him, not being killed himself.

Unreasonable orders would be ignored, and if obeying them meant death, they would be defied.

A chieftain did not have the right to order warriors to die.

He could order them to fight, to not flee — but how each warrior used his single life in that fight was the warrior's domain.

It was left to each individual.

Everyone valued their own life.

That they risked that life on the battlefield was entirely for the sake of honor.

Where one chose to die was a warrior's decision, and any chieftain who robbed them of that right and forced them into a dog's death would find his head on a different set of shoulders by the next morning.

They were by no means fools — rather, they were simply, genuinely human.

The military structures that would become standard in later eras were mechanisms of brainwashing, transforming people into organizational components by constraining individual free will.

Because of that, commanders could give orders at will and move armies freely — but the notion that one had the right to freely use and control something as irreplaceable as a person's life was supremely arrogant and cold-blooded.

Even if someone conceived such a notion, the question of whether it would be readily accepted was a firm no, and so there was no point in devising plans on paper.

Mustering numbers, strengthening individuals, improving coordination — that was their best.

In the sense of letting people fight as people, their current state was by no means wrong.

* * *

"……Incredible."

The word slipped from Godeus inside the shield wall.

What filled the air was the sound of countless arrows striking shields.

Against the advancing Garkha forces, Bernaik had adopted a strange formation.

Large plank shields were set up at intervals across the front line, warriors standing in formation behind them — and from the gaps between, hunters were loosing volley after volley of arrows.

Caught off guard by the fierce arrow storm right at the opening, they had taken some casualties before the men could cluster together and form their shield wall — but the dominant impression was one of surprise.

This was not the Aldos way of fighting — sword raised, charging headlong.

The large stationary shields were presumably a precaution against their throwing axes.

Since no man held them, they could be made thick enough to stop an axe.

Their opening move had been completely neutralized.

The throwing axe was not merely a weapon.

At the start of battle, when everyone was tense, it served a role in loosening stiff bodies and minds — Godeus considered that its most important function.

None of the warriors wanted to be seen as cowards.

Many claimed that killing enemies and being drenched in their blood from head to toe was a warrior's joy — but in truth, there was always the fear that one might die.

Brave words, loudly proclaimed, served to cut off their own escape, telling themselves that fighting like warriors and surviving was their only option.

Once the killing began, excitement would drive the fear away, but in the moments before battle, fear swelled the most.

The roaring war cries also served to crush the clinging dread.

The throwing axe was another such release.

A volley that caused even minor disruption put them at an advantage.

If one's own axe happened to fell a foe, the surge of *got the first one* was exhilarating, and even a miss that made the enemy flinch let a man tell himself the tide was in his favor.

A hundred men throwing might only kill a handful.

If the enemy took cover, sometimes as few as ten at the front went down.

But at that most tense moment — the charge — seeing brave enemies in the front rank fall to death gave the heart a tremendous respite.

Fear flipped to elation, and once the killing frenzy took hold, all was well.

Just before blades crossed, they could seize a great psychological advantage.

Battle — group battle especially — was not a contest of arms.

It was not simply a matter of which side was stronger, but a contest of the heart, a battle against fear.

The side that imposed fear won; the side that flinched lost.

Just as a single person wielding a sword could dominate a field through terror, what must be seized was not situational advantage but psychological advantage.

The side that made the enemy believe *we're losing* — was the side that won.

In that respect, having their opening volley of axes neutralized made this situation quite dire.

Above all, that strange formation — the fact that the enemy had made it work was what astonished Godeus most.

The famously fierce warriors of Aldos — surely they would never meekly comply with an order to *hide behind those planks to block the axes*.

The actual effect was modest at best.

Throwing axes were that kind of weapon; against Aldos warriors, more than a few would block them with their swords alone.

In terms of casualties, it was a number that could be accepted as a necessary cost.

If told they had nothing to fear from throwing axes and should advance, Aldos warriors would scoff and do exactly that — and indeed, on their honor, they would charge headlong into the teeth of it.

Aldos warriors charging with swords raised, unfazed by thrown axes — they were the very symbol of terror.

How to seize the advantage in that opening exchange — that was what would decide this battle.

Having steeled himself for precisely that, this situation was, in a certain sense, anticlimactic.

Aldos — using plank shields as walls and shooting arrows.

In return for neutralizing his opening axes, the enemy had also surrendered their own opening strike.

Aldos was terrifying precisely because they charged head-on, swords raised, without any tricks.

The sight of warriors who overcame the apparent suicidal folly of a direct charge through daily training and sheer willpower — that froze the spine of anyone who knew the terror of the battlefield.

No matter what you did, the enemy would overpower you with the sword alone.

Being made to believe that — *that* was Aldos's greatest strength.

Whether surrendering that in order to hide behind planks had any meaning — was questionable.

And yet, the fact that the enemy had made this work — that, more than anything, astonished Godeus.

This demonstrated that Bernaik was a man who commanded sufficient respect from his warriors to force them into a style of fighting against their will.

To bend the culture, beliefs, and traditions built over generations — yet still be tolerated. This was a scene that could only occur if the warriors' respect was that immense.

He was said to be only in his mid-twenties.

Bernaik, roughly the same age as Godeus — to most warriors, he was a youngster.

If Godeus had been in Bernaik's position, forcing the same thing upon his men would have been flatly impossible.

He would be laughed at as a coward and, depending on the case, deposed.

That was precisely why this situation inspired both astonishment and — with it — admiration.

At that young age, commanding warriors freely and bending them to his will — Bernaik was no ordinary warrior.

# Chapter 369: Alberan V

Left side forward, great shield to the front. His right hand let the axe hang loosely in a soft grip. The more one tensed, the more one became enslaved by one's own weapon.

Godevus spun his body on the spot. Joints screaming in protest, he hurled a right-handed axe stroke behind him.

Bernaik had vaulted over the great shield on his back, landing with breathtaking grace, and drove his gauntleted fist into the haft — at the base of the axe-head — timing it with his forward step.

The axe stopped dead. The impact reverberated through Godevus's grip as though he had struck a boulder, and in the instant before his body was entangled along with the axe, he released it and spun. The great shield, held horizontal, swung at the man's neck — but Bernaik caught up the axe with a flick of his wrist and leapt. He landed on the great shield, pressing Godevus flat to the earth, and leveled the axe's edge at his throat.

"……It is too soon for you to go to the halls. You will come with me."

His breath was not even slightly quickened. A voice of singular allure — cool, yet clinging, heavy.

Then he thrust the axe into the ground before Godevus, turned his back with perfect composure, and addressed the warriors who had been watching the duel.

"Alberan has set foot upon this land, and we of Aldos have chosen to become her sword."

At those words, Godevus and his men stared wide-eyed.

"In time, a nation of the divine child shall be built upon this land. To us who cower in the shadows, fearing the dragon — Alberan cleaves the gloom and shows us the light."

Bernaik indicated the sword hanging at his hip.

"……Gather beneath Alberan. If the sword of Aldos is fit to be offered to the divine child's right hand, then the shield of Garkha is fit to be offered to her left."

Then Bernaik turned to face the rear. Not toward Godevus, who had pushed himself upright to look at him, but toward the trees behind.

Suddenly, from one of those trees, a single girl alighted. Leaping from a high branch, she descended as though a feather were falling — soundless. Her knees absorbed the landing softly, and she stood.

The hem of the long robe that fluttered around her was out of place on a battlefield; long silver hair and purple eyes that held light like jewels surveyed the warriors in silence.

To those watching, her movements were so light — as though she possessed not weight, nor even substance — and so out of place in their beauty. Descending with the word *Alberan*, she was cloaked in illusion and mystery.

Bazarishe directed a mischievous smile at Bernaik, mouthing only *You noticed me, well done* — and an unamused Bernaik paid her no heed.

For her, this was the first step — the one she could not afford to fail. She was not the kind of person to leave it to others and wait in the rear. She would come to observe Bernaik's fight, doubling as an assessment.

A moment's thought was all it took to realize.

As though following a scripted performance, Bernaik drew his sword, knelt on one knee, and offered it to her. Bazarishe walked forward at her leisure, took the sword in hand, and raised its point toward the heavens.

"As Alberan, let me pose a question to you."

Her voice, too, was clear and cool. A child's voice that reached the ears as though it could pierce them — and yet possessed an indescribable bewitching quality, as intimate as a whisper even at a distance.

"Will you walk the sunlit path with me — or rot and crumble in the shadows?"

Then, slowly, the point leveled forward.

"Choose now, in this very moment. There are only two paths before you."

*Choose*, she continued, and one warrior dropped to his knee.

Seeing the man bow his head, the one beside him knelt as well, and the wave spread — friend and foe alike.

Watching this, Bazarishe narrowed her eyes, then spun around and smiled at Godevus.

"Great chieftain of Garkha — Godevus."

"……"

The girl said nothing more, and simply extended her left hand — the one that held no sword.

Godevus hesitated for a moment, then knelt before her, bowed his head, and raised his great shield in both hands as though presenting it.

Bazarishe accepted the great shield, and her smile deepened.

"Let us be on good terms from now on, shall we? As those who walk together."

In response, Godevus lowered his head another degree.

And so the battle ended, and the dead were buried.

The dead were overwhelmingly on Garkha's side, but by Bazarishe's order, both sides cooperated — digging pits and laying the warriors' remains within them.

Bernaik watched from a distance, and beside him, Bazarishe laughed.

"Hmhm, I've grown even fonder of you, Bernaik. You fulfilled my every requirement — eliminating the ones we didn't need exactly as requested, with minimal casualties on both sides…… You kept your promise not to draw your sword, and met all my demands."

The ones *we didn't need* — her gaze fell on the warriors who had held the center. Having lost their two leaders, Almas and Rottia, and suffered no small casualties, some among them shed tears, mourning the loss.

Even so, she wore her smile throughout.

"I acknowledge they needed to be dealt with. But whatever the case, they were warriors. I won't tell you to grieve, but know this: laugh at their deaths and you will make enemies."

"It's all so complicated, isn't it……"

"The word *Alberan* that serves you so well is built upon a foundation of faith. A sharp sword, mishandled, takes its wielder's life…… If you claim to be a god yourself, that's another matter — but if not, make the effort to learn."

When no other eyes were on them, Bernaik dispensed with formal speech. Bazarishe was undeniably a genius, stronger than anyone — but she was no warrior. She did not even know what reverence for a warrior's faith meant, and naturally, she could not understand the reverence directed at her by others. She was still a child.

Showing deference to Bazarishe was like giving jewels to a dog. Bernaik despised such meaningless gestures.

Bazarishe glared at Bernaik with some displeasure and pouted — *But I don't understand these things*.

"I can see that a good person dying is indeed a shame. But showing pity to one's enemies like that — it's incomprehensible to me."

"That's because, at root, you think of nothing but yourself."

"……?"

"A person is an individual, but also a member of a group. The higher one's perspective rises, the more one thinks of the group's wellbeing."

Individual likes and dislikes are just personal, he said.

"They may have disagreed with me, but both were decent men as warriors of the group. They swung their swords not for their own desires, but for Aldos. The deaths of two men who defied their chieftain were inevitable…… but that does not change the fact that they were fine warriors."

Bazarishe's petulant expression went blank, and she simply stared up at him. Those large purple eyes gazed with empty, inorganic light.

"The same applies to enemies. It is only natural to kill someone who became your foe and raised a blade against you. But that does not diminish their worth. A warrior is still a warrior, and as long as that is so, their value does not decrease. …… Even if they were my enemy as the leader of Aldos, seen from the heavens above — within the great mass of humanity — they were still warriors who deserved to be honored."

Bazarishe considered for a moment and said, *I see*.

"I think I understand — a little. And you explain things quite well, Bernaik."

"Raising you is not my job, but……"

"I'll have you know, I'm well aware that *raising* is a terribly rude word."

She glared at him with displeasure, but he continued, unperturbed.

"Look at what aligns with what you're aiming for. If you want to become a god, you need learn nothing at all. ……But if you wish to join the mass of humanity, much learning is required."

"Muu……"

"I said it because I took you for the latter — but how you think about it is up to you. For the rest, go ask that man who's supposed to be raising you."

He jerked his chin toward Mitskronia, approaching from behind.

His armor was drenched in blood. It had been Mitskronia and Aldos warriors loyal to Bernaik who had charged into the Garkha rear.

Allowing the weakened center to be pushed in, advancing the spear-equipped flanks forward — forming a double envelopment, with Mitskronia lying in wait at a distance, moving in on the horn signal to deliver the finishing blow. That was the shape of this battle.

The one surprise had been that the archers alone had not been enough — but credit was due to Godevus's quick thinking.

Seeing Mitskronia return from overseeing the burial rites, and similarly watching Godevus return from keeping watch over the Garkha warriors, Bernaik began to walk.

"Bernaik."

"……Ask Mitskronia for the rest."

"What would you have done if the dueling opponent had been someone stronger?"

He furrowed his brow and turned back. Bazarishe's face was blank. Like a doll, she simply stared up at him.

"You're the one who told me not to draw my sword."

"Then, even if it meant being killed, you would not have drawn it?"

"Then I would simply have been that kind of man."

Bernaik said it as though it were the most natural thing in the world.

"……Whatever you may be, I have acknowledged you as Alberan and sworn upon my sword and my name. A man who would bend that — I would not acknowledge as a warrior."

"You really are a fool, aren't you."

"Say what you like. Whatever you might be, I am who I am."

Bazarishe stared at Bernaik for a long moment, then laughed — like a child.

Then — *In that case, it's an order* — she said.

"From now on, even if I command it, you will not die without permission. If you are about to die, you will break my orders and prioritize staying alive."

Bazarishe held up a finger and smiled.

"……I want to talk with you more, Bernaik."

To those words, Bernaik answered with exasperation — *I am deeply honored* — and turned his back.

"Therefore it is an order. You will not try to wander off somewhere on your own, but stay here and talk to me about all manner of things."

"…………"

"Surely you wouldn't bend your oath upon sword and name over something like this, Bernaik?"

He turned to look at the delighted Bazarishe once more, expression sour, then shifted his gaze to the approaching Mitskronia.

"What seems to be the matter, Lord Bernaik?"

"……You are this girl's handler, Mitskronia. Do something about her before my patience reaches its limit."
