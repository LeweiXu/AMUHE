# Chapter 331: Super Galaxy Queen ☆ Krischeria ~The Beloved Princess With Excessive Destiny-Force Just Goes On Being Happy in a Kindly World~

Thank you for enjoying *A Maiden's Unwanted Heroic Epic*.

Approximately three years have passed since its completion.

Reading back through the work again, I have come to feel that *A Maiden's Unwanted Heroic Epic* is, after all, a piece carrying many problems.

Setting aside typos and unattended-to bad prose, the cruel depictions and structure are conspicuous in places, the protagonists keep grinding through inconclusive inner conflicts every time, and there isn't a shred of pop sensibility to it.

Suffering is precisely where a story's depth and interest reside.

What appeal could there possibly be in a tale of characters with no inner conflict?

It is from such considerations that I deliberately built it as that sort of work, but on review the whole thing has indeed turned out to be an extraordinarily dark, gloomy story.

In the end, this work arrives at an unprecedented and frankly idiotic-seeming conclusion that ought to be called

"Yuri-Yuri ☆ Happy Eternal End ☆,"

in which seven beautiful girls live forever in yuri-yuri bliss — but many readers are thrown off by the misery along the way, and that very ending stands atop a great many sacrifices.

Many people who, with a different first encounter, might never have died and could have cooperated with the protagonists and built good relationships with them, ended up dying.

Just a few buttons done up wrong.

The deaths of those men and women, dying for reasons written into the work itself, are too sad and too cruel.

Add this to the inner conflict above, and what arises is something to be called "(;_;)↓Synergy of Gloom↓(;_;)."

It paints over the work's sunny side, "o(^_^)o↑Mansion Yuri-Yuri Effect↑o(^_^)o," and the gloom wins out.

In this full remake, as far as possible, in order to scoop back up the people who got kicked aside for the sake of those girls' happiness, I plan to drastically improve the encounter aspect in particular — strengthening the "system in which characters appear and meet at exquisite timing and somehow get along nicely."

The aim is a full revision into "*A Maiden's Unwanted Heroic Epic in Which the Buttons Are Absolutely Never Done Up Wrong.*"

Also, the title *A Maiden's Unwanted Heroic Epic*, gloomy-looking from the very letters of it, is being changed to match.

"*Super Galaxy Queen ☆ Krischeria ~The Beloved Princess With Excessive Destiny-Force Just Goes On Being Happy in a Kindly World~*"

Please bear that in mind.

The revision is currently in progress, but some readers may be uneasy about what kind of story it will become.

For now, please enjoy the following digest version of the early portion as a sample of the atmosphere.

(※Important) Notice regarding the full remake of *A Maiden's Unwanted Heroic Epic*.

"Uncle and Niece: 1"

Setting aside many remonstrances, in the end his elder brother Sherbaza had apparently decided to raise the girl, who had been kept under house arrest, as a princess.

Was he so reluctant to lose the child of his concubine? It was an unusual thing for his earnest brother, Gildanstein thought.

The name given to her was Krischeria — a noble moon, waning and tracing an arc.

The names of the royal house must always be impeccable.

On that count, that name was discordant — a name carrying the guilt of breaking with custom.

"……Your Highness, my lord uncle."

"An uncle has come to see his niece, that's all. Don't go on guard. ……What are you doing?"

The servant had stiffened when he entered her chamber, but Gildanstein paid no mind and walked in.

Krischeria, now five, had taken a vase in her hands and was polishing it with a cloth.

Beautiful silver hair, jewel-like violet eyes. A white one-piece dress.

The way she expressionlessly tilted her small head apparently struck many people as unsettling.

Without anyone saying so aloud, those in the royal palace knew she was the despised child who had been kept in confinement; the pressure of those emotionless eyes likely drove the impression.

Eyes that watched every gesture of their interlocutor — like a snake's, if one were to compare them.

Of all the eyes Gildanstein had ever seen, he had never encountered any like them.

"……? Krische is wiping the vase, wipe-wipe."

He spoke, exasperated by the look that seemed to say, *isn't that obvious?*

"That much I can see. I was asking why you, a princess, are doing the cleaning."

"Hahh……"

"……My apologies, Your Highness, that is to say——"

"Enough. I'm used to it by now. ……Tea."

The black-haired servant with the unlucky-looking face bowed deeply and brewed black tea.

While Krischeria continued to polish the vase, Gildanstein propped his cheek on a hand at the seat by the window.

A strange girl — perhaps it was because she had been confined in the tower, or perhaps it had been there from the start.

When she finished wiping the vase, she let a small satisfied smile float across her face, then came pattering over toward him.

"Did you have something to discuss with Krische?"

"I came to see your face just to kill some boredom. Don't shorten your name."

"Krischeria is too long…… Tou-sama said it was fine to use that."

Out of guilt of some sort, his brother was tremendously soft on Krischeria.

"……Enough. Lecturing you is less rewarding than teaching a dog."

"Krische is smarter than a dog."

"So you are. Apparently too smart for me to be your owner."

He patted her head loosely and Krischeria watched, the corners of her mouth softening.

Letters and arithmetic she was said to have absorbed in no time at all; her cleverness was not on the level of an ordinary person. Her younger sister, born after, was the same — at only two years of age she was learning from her elder sister and picking it all up in no time.

"Genius" was an inadequate word for how clever she was.

That was likely one of the reasons people found her uncanny.

Most people felt that way toward beings beyond their understanding.

Some loved the two of them, but most kept the pair at a distance and watched from afar.

He set his lips to the black tea the servant called Nora had set down, and for a while the topic was tutors.

There seemed to be no one capable of properly educating them; two tutors had already, in no time, requested reassignment.

Being too clever was its own problem — they couldn't even hold a proper conversation.

Especially scholars, who had high pride and often delicate temperaments.

The arithmetic instructor, having seen her solve at a glance the proof he had taken on as the lifelong proposition of his career, seemed to have come down with an illness of the heart.

"The problem is rather that worthless scholars were appointed as your tutors. I'll speak to my brother. In their place, read your way through the palace library from end to end. If you go at it earnestly, two or three years will be enough for you."

"Books……"

"I'm not telling you to *understand* it all. The parts you don't understand, just memorize. Ask my brother later. When I have time, I'll teach you what I can. ……Books will serve you better than tutors."

"Hmm, that may indeed be so. Once Krische finishes reading them, is that the end?"

"Of academic learning, yes. For things that can't be learned from books — horsemanship and the like — keep on with the tutors."

Krischeria made an unmistakably reluctant face.

"What is it? If you dislike the tutor I can change them."

"……Riding hurts Krische's bottom. Running seems faster."

"Endure it, at least somewhat. It costs you nothing to know. Over long distances, a horse is convenient."

Krischeria had already mastered physical enhancement perfectly.

Beyond mere intelligence, she was a head and shoulders above in every faculty.

If only she weren't a fool besides, there would be nothing to say, but perhaps no one is perfect.

"After that — self-defense…… If you want to learn the use of a sword and so on, I'll teach you, but only after these other things. You're still too small in body, and my brother would dislike it."

"Should Krische learn it as well?"

"I think so. Born into the royal family, you can never know when your life will be sought. Learning to defend yourself can hardly hurt. You'll pick it up quickly."

"Indeed, that may well be so……"

She tilted her head this way and that, considered, and nodded.

Then, picking up a cookie, she stared fixedly at him.

"……?"

"It's nothing. Cookies — do you not like them?"

"It isn't that I dislike them…… nor am I particularly fond."

He frowned at the meaning of her question and looked at Nora, who bowed her head in fright.

"Today's cookies, Your Highness — that is, Princess Krischeria… Her lessons ended early and she had time, and I thought perhaps if there were anything I could teach her…… My apologies."

"I see. Don't be so frightened. I have no intention of doing anything to you or to her."

"……I am sorry."

A tiresome woman in every detail.

She seemed terribly afraid of Gildanstein, flinching at his every motion.

Krischeria was glancing back and forth between Nora and him; he sighed, picked up a cookie, and put it in his mouth.

The taste of an utterly unremarkable cookie, slightly sweeter than what he usually ate, spread through his mouth.

"Ehehe, was it good?"

"Ordinary. Not bad, but cloyingly sweet."

"Mmh…… Krische felt it had come out terribly well. Kreschenta said it was delicious too."

"Then your sense of taste is the thing that's off."

At his answer, Krischeria again tilted her head, mused alone, and ate a cookie.

Watching her, he decided his business was done and was just rising to leave when, from the bed, there came a rustling — and his niece Kreschenta sat up.

Rubbing her sleepy eyes, she stood, and seeing Gildanstein she made a dubious face — and as a matter of course leapt up onto her elder sister's lap.

She fixed him with a glare, saying nothing — this was always the way of her.

"I came to see my niece's face, that's all."

"…………"

At the words she turned her face pointedly aside and clung tightly, hugging Krischeria.

Still only two — and like Krischeria, a baby who never cried.

He knew she could already speak fluently, but unlike her sister she was inward; with anyone besides Krischeria and Nora she scarcely spoke at all.

In appearance she was a small child, but in temperament she looked somewhat more proper than Krischeria.

"Kreschenta, you must greet him properly."

"……, hello."

"You greet someone with your eyes on them. Until you can greet him properly, no cookies."

At Krischeria's words she pursed her lips and reluctantly, looking away, said *hello.*

Krischeria gave a small *mmh* of disapproval, then took a cookie and gave it to her sister.

"Here, that's fine — but in front of others, take a more befitting, endearing manner. Whether or not the two of you are as you appear is no concern of mine, but there are those who'd dislike it."

Kreschenta glanced sidelong at him and clung once more to her sister.

"……But Krische *is* as she appears."

"Yes, in a sense you are."

"In a sense……"

In intellect and capability she was outstanding, but in temperament she was a docile five-year-old, just as she looked.

She held the question in mind for a moment, then evidently it ceased to matter.

Stroking her sister's head, with a vacant expression as if she'd just remembered something, she asked—

"Ah, that reminds me, there was something Krische wanted to ask."

"Something to ask?"

"Yes. Word is that Uncle takes pleasure in tormenting innocent people——"

"——Lady Krischeria!"

"Mghh?"

In a flustered hurry Nora pressed her hand over Krischeria's mouth.

And bowed her head in fright.

"M-my apologies, Your Highness, that is——"

"Let her go. I don't mind. ……What of it?"

Released, Krischeria looked troubled, glanced at Nora, then back at Gildanstein.

"It's just, Uncle has rather lost his reputation over that, and Krische, who is friendly with him, feels her own reputation is being dragged down by association. Honestly, Krische would very much like you to stop."

"That your head is not on straight is plain fact. It isn't all my fault."

"Mmh…… Even so. Surely the fewer reasons your reputation drops, the better."

Krischeria spoke in displeasure, and asked—

"As far as Krische is concerned, if Uncle has some reason for it, Krische won't insist, but if it isn't strictly necessary it ought to stop. ……Is there some good that comes of doing it?"

"Not particularly. ……At a stretch, one obtains various pieces of information."

"Information……"

"Hurting them makes them speak of various things, and merchants who come selling things know I'm a good customer and pass me their information. Well, it's a side-benefit to the play…… in any case, since around the time you were born, I've grown bored with it. That sort of play is no more frequent than the average."

At first it had simply been torture.

He would lay hands on those who had broken the law and vent his various irritations upon them.

When that ran out, with information-gathering as the pretext, he would buy slaves brought in from other countries and repeat the same.

Before he knew it, the torture itself had become the point; he would buy the slaves who interested him and spend hours absorbed in faces one could not ordinarily see.

Mere criminals. The arrogant, the abject, those merely unfortunate, the strong-of-justice incompetents and fools.

Only at the extremity did people show Gildanstein their true faces.

Those who said *do as you like* and, when their hands and feet were being cut off, begged for mercy.

Those who said *I won't talk* and broke at the third nail.

Or those who cursed Gildanstein as a rotten degenerate and, even driven mad, kept that posture unchanged.

Those who would die for an ideal, holding to a noble's soul.

That, surely, was what he had wanted to see.

Around then the play had become routine.

Just by looking into those eyes and exchanging a few words, he could tell them apart before the play even began.

There is no game so dull as one whose answer one already knows.

"Then, have you stopped now?"

"At most I buy a slave I've taken a fancy to and keep them at the manor. I attend slave duels and shows now and then out of social obligation, but only that much for entertainment…… If you're interested, will you come along next time?"

"Krische will not. Just to be clear, Krische is *talking* about how Uncle's strange games drag down Krische's reputation."

She frowned and glared at him.

"If you say you've stopped, that's well enough. If it's only by way of obligation, perhaps in various respects there's no helping it. But please, at least, do your best not to let strange rumors arise. If your reputation gets any worse and Krische's drops along with it, Krische will sever ties with you."

"What an ungrateful child you are."

"It's because Krische thinks you've been good to her up to now that she's saying this for your sake. Surely, rather than being hated by others, working to be liked is far better…… isn't that right, Nora?"

"E-eh, um……"

Nora, unable to interpose, let her eyes wander in fright; Krischeria tilted her head.

Kreschenta yawned sleepily and watched her sister with an exasperated look.

"Why should it matter to be liked by the rabble?"

"……? Surely there's no comparison to be made between being liked and being disliked. Being disliked comes back to you as harm; being liked comes back as benefit."

Plain and simple, the girl described the world she saw.

In her crystal-clear violet eyes there floated not a doubt.

Without the complicated reasoning and logic of nobility, royalty, or humanity, she divided the world in two with the words *benefit* and *harm.*

*A refreshingly pure fool* — that was what one thought, watching her.

"In Krische's experience this investment is highly profitable. The rate of return is extremely high. Krische is surprised that Uncle didn't already understand it…… Ah, that's right."

*Uncle,* she said, and beckoned with little waves of her hand.

When Gildanstein frowned and approached, she beckoned further.

Before he could ask what she meant, her small hand touched his head and began to move.

Nora was speechless.

"……What is this?"

"A reward. If Uncle does his best, Krische shall reward him with pat-pats. Krische will also do her best to bake cookies you'll find delicious. ……A clear, easy-to-understand reward, surely, leads to motivation."

"Don't lump me in with you and Kreschenta."

Without anger so much as exasperation, he caught Krischeria's hand and pulled it away.

*Mmh,* Krischeria said, considering.

"……Well, I understand your argument. I'll consider it."

"Hmm, just considering won't do — Krische wants you to do your best…… Ah, Krische is also planning to bake a cake next time. She'll let Uncle eat some too."

"I don't want it."

"Uncle and Niece: 2"

Some time after that, he attended a feast of the dregs.

Dregs flattering this way and that, and he himself watching that mad revelry idly.

A tedious time it was.

When boredom came on, he would usually order someone to dispel it for him, but this time even that desire failed to stir.

Without his noticing, his thoughts had turned to Krischeria.

What to teach her next, his thinking ran; thinking that, his thoughts then ran to what he himself wanted to make of her.

What in fact did he himself wish to do?

A question he could think on without arriving anywhere.

Yet the boredom of before was now, somehow, gone.

As far as time-killing went, it was far superior — a play he did not tire of.

* * *

"……What is this, Gil?"

"A roster, as you can read for yourself, brother. What each of them has been doing is summarized too. Dispose of them as you see fit. They're skilled at hiding their tails — if you act, act all at once."

The king's chamber — along the walls, armor and swords and paintings of battlefields were displayed.

Sherbaza Alberan, like Gildanstein, sighed and swept up his golden hair with one hand.

Their faces were said to closely resemble each other.

Their builds were the same; the only difference was a beard, present or absent.

"I am asking what you intend by this."

"I've simply tired of them. I'm weary of dealing with them. Don't worry — pieces useful to me I've kept aside; do as you like with the rest."

He poured the wine he had brought into a glass for his brother; Sherbaza gazed at it.

Without minding, he poured one for himself, took a sip.

A truly fine wine.

"……Surely, Gil, you don't mean that for this——"

"Heh, your weakness is that you're too soft, brother. Do you still trust me even now? ……Drink. It's a label I haven't tried before, but for the price it's not a bad wine."

Sherbaza sighed, then quietly took the wine in his mouth and swallowed.

And said—

"I do not believe you are rotten to the core, a degenerate. ……Had a few things been even slightly different, it would not have come to this."

"There are no *might-have-beens* in the past, brother. All of it was inevitable…… what was meant to happen, happened."

"A choice at the end of doubt does not necessarily turn out the same. I still believe, even now, that you were the one fit to be king. And it was not only I — at the time, many thought so. ……But I hesitated."

He spoke as if mocking himself, and stood.

And took down the king's sword Bezelia from its place on the wall.

"My hesitation arose from weakness. I did not want to acknowledge that I was no vessel for kingship. *It is too soon to give up; if I work at it, I might still——* and so I deferred the single word: that I would yield the right of succession to you."

He drew the great sword that only the king is permitted to wield, and gazed at it.

Sherbaza's face was reflected in the beautiful blade.

"The king's sword Bezelia, the sword consecrated to the founder-king Bazarishe. Its beautiful blade represents the unclouded heart, and at the pommel, the clear violet eye that pursues the ideal. ……I would gaze at our father's sword in secret and look at myself reflected in it. Even now, when I gaze, my face clouds the steel."

Returning the sword to its sheath, Sherbaza returned to his seat and continued.

"……More devoted to the ideal than any, never doubting that to assist our father and me was your duty…… If anyone is the soft-hearted one, Gil, it is *you.* I was not born with as pure a heart as yours."

"It's true I'll not deny that the me of those days was a fool."

He answered with a laugh, and Sherbaza watched the surface his wine traced.

"……It's because you were so devoted to the ideal that I can understand the depth of your disillusionment. By rights, I ought to have spoken the contents of my heart to you before it came to that. My foolish, viscous feelings…… would, in time, have faded before the figure of you become a proper king, and I would not now be sitting here regretting it."

"There's no use speaking of a past that won't return."

"Indeed. ……But the future, perhaps, can still be changed."

Sherbaza emptied his wine and lifted the glass.

Gildanstein poured.

"……The many words you once swore to me, I have not forgotten even now. And neither have you. Even repeating your degenerate amusements, you have fulfilled your duty as king's brother, and you have supported me."

"I'm doing only enough work not to have my head taken off."

"That is not so. ……However much you have despaired of the world, you do what you do for one purpose only — not to soil the words you swore on your name. ……Even so, I am your brother. I know that, whatever happens, you are a man who can never throw away the last pride of a noble."

*——And that is exactly why, even envying, longing for, hating you, I still cannot kill you.*

Sherbaza spoke quietly, and laughed.

"Watching those two, I am reminded of you in your younger days. The resemblance is strong."

"……That's an insult past insult, that one."

"I don't mean it to mock. ……Why a baby who does not cry is called a despised child — watching you and watching them, I came to understand. The world is simply too sullied for those who are too pure to live in."

And, looking up as if at the ceiling, he continued.

"……I'm glad I avoided yet another foolish choice. That you came to see me today — surely the spark for it was those two, was it not? Looking at you, I can tell."

"Interpret it as you like."

He tipped his glass, and into the empty glass Sherbaza poured for him.

And, emptying his own as well, Sherbaza smiled.

"Truly, this is a fine wine. ……Next time, come empty-handed. I'll have my treasured wine brought out."

"……Brother's taste is rather lacking, so I won't get my hopes up."

"That assessment, I'll have you reconsider on the next occasion."

Sherbaza said it with a laugh, and brought the wine to his lips.

"……Those two, I leave to you. Show them what you wish to show, what you have wanted to see. In time, surely, they will show you the scene you sought from the bottom of your heart."

"Don't blame me if the country splits."

"Entrusted to the you of now, surely it will not come to that."

Eyes closed, Sherbaza smiled and said it.

"Uncle and Niece: 3"

Royalty have the duty of attending many ceremonies, and most of them were tedious.

But of them, the victory feast alone was something he did not dislike.

He would read the battlefield records sent in, and picture in his head what kind of battle it had been.

How the soldiers had fought; how the commanders had agonized and reached what decisions.

And how it had come to its conclusion.

While composing the words he would speak at the victory ceremony, he listened to the steel echoes the warriors played, the discord of life and death.

There were also those, like Dagren Garkha, who sent in records too curt to be of any interest, but this time the main theater had been the west.

Aulgorn Hilkintos and Elderant.

In terms of how he fought, Aulgorn — who took no risks — was not to his taste, but the over-ornamented, detail-heavy records were not bad as reading.

The amusing thing was that, taken on their own, they made him look like an undisputed master of war.

As a general entrusted with one of the four cardinal directions, Aulgorn was a man of sufficient talent.

Only — he was thorough about winning what he could be sure to win, avoiding direct confrontation until the chance of losing had been painted over, and he despised any blot on his undefeated record too dearly.

The west, sacrificial to that disease, was always troubled; though by rights it was reasonably rich land, of the four directions it produced the least tax revenue.

He spoke of those sacrifices in such phrases as *the violations inflicted upon the people by an enemy army that launched a contemptible surprise attack,* and *hearing the cries of the pitiful people occupied and oppressed,* he would *launch a desperate counter-offensive* and succeed in *bringing the iron hammer of justice down upon evil barbarian hordes possessed of vast forces*— that was the reliable structure.

This time, Aulgorn had bought time by retreating until Gildanstein arrived.

At the moment Bogan, charging hard from the north, had cut off the enemy's rear, Gildanstein and he together had turned and counter-attacked.

The matter was settled when Gildanstein took the enemy general's head.

That kind of give-and-take he understood; by yielding glory to Gildanstein and to the central generals, Aulgorn arranged for accommodations regarding "sacrifices" — that was the usual way.

This time too, in a tone as if he had been right there at the scene as Gildanstein took the enemy commander's head, with truly inflammatory language he had recorded *the vigorous heroics of His Highness,* in such a vein that even Gildanstein himself, the subject, was left exasperated.

Glory, or the petty self-preservation of small men — neither concerned him.

Being lifted up too far made his skin crawl, and the goggling eyes were not to his liking.

*I'll dispose of him on some pretext like neglecting the protection of the people,* he would think on the road west each time, but once the battlefield play was over, Gildanstein was always in a good mood.

That, in the end, he was inclined to forgive a little unpleasantness, perhaps showed that this man too was, surprisingly, a capable hand.

* * *

"You two — what are you doing?"

"……? Krische is braiding Kreschenta and the beard."

"Hahaha, it seems my beard has greatly taken Their Highnesses' fancy."

The victory ceremony had ended; this was the feast.

Looking for the two of them, he found them in a corner braiding Felwors's long white beard.

Splitting the beard left and right, Krischeria in a black dress and Kreschenta in a white dress were each twisting a side into braids; Felwors was laughing pleasantly.

"Ehehe, Kreschenta, you're good at it."

"Onee-sama is good at teaching."

"As expected of Krische's little sister. Krische shall pat-pat you later."

"Yes……!"

The kingdom's First Princess and Second Princess — clad in elegant dresses, twisting and twisting at the beard, the two of them left Gildanstein exasperated.

Nine and six; for all that they had grown somewhat in appearance, they were as foolish as ever.

Even Kreschenta, who had been inward and seemed to have somewhat more proper sensibilities than Krischeria, was day by day walking the same fool's path as her sister.

"Uncle?"

"……Whatever do you want?"

He took the two by the necks and pulled them away, and told Felwors—

"Don't indulge them. What do you mean to do if they get any more foolish than this?"

"My apologies, Your Highness. It was so endearing somehow that I quite——"

"……Both Onee-sama and I are wiser than Uncle."

"However wise you are, if to outside eyes you are a fool, then a fool you are. Have a little awareness of your station as a princess."

Kreschenta glared at him and puffed her cheeks in displeasure.

"Come. There's someone to greet."

"Understood. ……Kreschenta, you mustn't get angry like that. Uncle is short-tempered, so it can't be helped. We must take that as a counter-example to learn from."

"Mmh…… very well."

"Yes. What a good child you are. You mustn't grow up to be like Uncle."

He pushed the foolish conversation behind him out of his attention.

Like animal cries — there was no use being angry at each utterance.

Pushing through the crowd that way, he reached his target.

"Christand. Krischeria and Kreschenta."

"Well, then…… It is my first honor to meet you, Your Highnesses."

Bogan Christand dropped to one knee, and his wife Lazura, with red hair gathered up and wearing an indigo dress, took the hem of her skirt and gave a standing curtsy.

Even the usually foolish two understood the etiquette to that level, and matched, like a textbook, an ideal curtsy.

At the perfect, unjarring synchrony of the two from start to finish, Bogan briefly knit his brows; Lazura's cat-like eyes went round.

"I am Bogan Christand, and this is my wife Lazura. Though my abilities pale beside those of my great predecessors, I have been entrusted with the protection of the north as one of the kingdom's four-direction generals."

"Ah, well…… Krische knows. The brilliant feats of arms you have accumulated — Krische, as First Princess, is very glad that a warrior such as you has staked life and body in fulfilling your duties for the kingdom. In this latest action, your performance was splendid."

"Words too kind for my station."

Having recited what was half a set phrase, Krischeria glanced sidelong at Gildanstein.

*We've made our greetings, may we go home now?* — he ignored the glance entirely.

"I particularly wanted you to meet her. As you may know, I had her come along recently, and on a trial basis I had her draft the operational plan. You found it easy to move, didn't you?"

"Yes, Your Highness. ……I had heard rumor, but…"

At the figure of a girl who was nothing more than a child, Bogan was perplexed; Gildanstein laughed and told him—

"This is the Reine of our age, Bogan. As you can see, she is a brat, but I have no hesitation in calling her a genius."

"Reine……"

"For today, take it as no more than an introduction. In time I will bring her to your side as well."

"……My lord."

And he told Krischeria—

"This man is the general I trust most, and a warrior. He will be a help to you in the future. Remember him well."

"Hahh…… A pleasure to make your acquaintance. The other day, thanks to General going around behind so quickly, things ended quickly, and Krische would be very glad if you keep up that sort of work."

The moment the set phrases were over, she was as usual.

Gildanstein had given up.

"No, my own performance was thanks to fine subordinates and the supporting attack of the central army…… that strategic maneuver — was that also Princess Krischeria's design?"

"Eh, well…… do you mean when the enemy was on two fronts?"

"Yes."

"Then yes. Thanks to your moving so swiftly, we encircled them cleanly."

"Mm……as a squad leader, you pass."

"Indeed — Koza has a sharp eye. With further training, he could amount to something."

After Krische-sama explained the unit's purpose once more, we were called in one by one and asked basic questions about tactics. None of us knew the first thing about tactics; the questions were geared more toward testing imagination and quickness of thought.

At the time, Krische-sama was, to us, an evil noblewoman. Being told "you pass" in that condescending way grated, but no one was about to pick a fight with someone the captain showed deference to.

The captain was a good man by any measure — a model soldier. Exactly the kind of man you'd picture when you heard "centurion" — yet when it came to Krische-sama, he was almost excessively careful.

Was even a fine man like that weak before power, or was that simply what soldiers were? We didn't know Krische-sama's true ability at the time, and since we had too much exposure to her, we were in fact skeptical of her strength. Even when we heard rumors she was a monster, most men laughed it off.

Every time the captain bowed and scraped before Krische-sama, it left a bad taste in our mouths.

From there the unit was reorganized, and we moved on to combat-simulation training in the forest. Having been subjected to nothing but hard labor, we were thrilled.

And what we discovered in that training was genuinely moving. In barely a month, the unit had transformed into something entirely different.

For a while our bodies were thrown off balance by the unfamiliar augmentation, but once we could wield it properly — the potential was staggering. Men like me who'd done rough work before could quit the military and go back to our old trade as top-tier hands with offers pouring in. I was certain I could take on several pre-augmentation versions of myself and send them packing.

And the crowning moment was a live-exercise bout against two light-infantry centuries selected for the reds. Two centuries — double our number — and we crushed them.

Their side was nearly wiped out; our side had seven confirmed kills.

Light infantry was the army's finest. Men chosen as "reds" were already proven swordsmen, and having overwhelmed them, we were ecstatic.

When the captain told us that Krische-sama would personally assess our abilities next, we went wild. The cruder fellows jabbered about ripping the mad rabbit's clothes in a staged accident, or beating her senseless and making her cry — the usual nonsense.

Koza was, if anything, on that side of things — I at least wanted to humiliate her and vent the frustration we'd built up — and most of us probably felt the same. That's how brutal Krische-sama's training had been. Even looking back now, I'd say the same, so there's no helping it.

But our opponent was none other than Krische-sama.

There's always someone better, and people are not created equal, and reality is a ruthless thing.

The very next day, we were made to understand that we were nothing more than nobodies with a little extra hair on them.

What is the most unreasonable thing in life?

If someone asked me that, I'd tell them this:

Being made to stand with a sword in your hand before the princess named Krische Christand. That's what.

# Chapter 333: The Dawn Crescent II

The next day, the young lady of the Christand household arrived at the forest training ground same as always. True to the rumor that she hated horses, she'd walked all the way there, wearing that listless expression.

The captain barked out a crisp salute and we followed suit; she returned it with barely a glance.

I'd thought she might at least congratulate us on our decisive victory — but of course, no such thing happened.

"Well, let's get started. Krische wouldn't mind taking you all on at once, but……Dagra, what do you think?"

"Ma'am. If possible, I'd ask that you work with us one squad at a time. I believe that would be easier for the men to learn from."

"Mm, I see……"

With that, Krische-sama turned her eyes on us for the first time. Probably because I happened to be nearest, she looked at me and beckoned.

"Second Squad, on the double! Everyone else, step back."

Dagra called out the orders, and in the cleared space, it was the five of us facing Krische-sama.

At that moment, I think I was still assuming she meant to have us do practice swings or something.

"Dagra, wooden sword."

"Ma'am."

The captain immediately presented his wooden sword to Krische-sama with both hands, then stepped back.

Spinning the wooden sword deftly in one hand, she said to me:

"Go ahead."

"……? 'Go ahead' meaning?"

"Koza! Answer like a soldier!"

"Y-yes, sir! My apologies, Captain! What does 'go ahead' mean, ma'am?!"

I hastily rephrased after the captain's bark, and Krische-sama tilted her head slightly.

*What is he even asking?* — that sort of look, as she traced her lips with a fingertip, thinking.

If anyone should have been tilting their head, it was us. The idea that she was ordering us to attack her five against one — such an insane command — hadn't even crossed our minds, and we exchanged glances.

"As I told you yesterday, the corps commander's adjutant will personally be putting you through your paces from now on. If you understand, draw your swords!"

"Y-yes, sir……wait, ma'am? You mean five against one?"

"That's right. ……My apologies, adjutant. Is that correct?"

"Yes. Krische was being unclear. ……So with that understood, whenever you're ready."

Even hearing it, confusion was the dominant feeling. We'd been told about this, true, but five against one — and our opponent was a child not even fifteen.

We'd talked big the day before, but having five grown men gang up on her felt a bit much.

"It's five against one, but you don't need to hold back. Come at Krische like you mean to kill her. Um, even if Krische gets hurt or dies, Krische swears on her name she won't complain."

She nodded to herself, matter-of-fact, and we exchanged glances again.

*What do we do?* — the others looked at me with their eyes, and with no other choice I drew the wooden sword from my belt and raised it.

"Hurting her is going too far."

I whispered it, and *right*, they nodded.

"Krische is saying you don't need to hold back, though……"

She shouldn't have been able to hear at that distance and volume, but what kind of ears did she have?

In hindsight, it was probably lip-reading. Krische-sama had frighteningly good eyes and could read words from the movement of lips alone.

"Well, fine. It's the first time, after all — let's get through this quickly, explanations and all."

Five ken between us and her.

It wasn't that I couldn't see it. I saw the moment Krische-sama lowered her stance. I saw the moment she lunged.

I just saw it, was all.

How to describe that strange sensation — it's difficult. I knew she'd lunged. My head understood I needed to block. But my body felt no danger whatsoever, and didn't so much as twitch.

Usually it was the opposite. If someone suddenly slashed at you, your body moved before your brain could think — instinctively pulling back, creating distance.

You didn't need to be a veteran of rough work. Even a complete amateur would flinch if someone jumped out from a shadow. Whether you froze or your legs gave out varied by person, but the body moved before thought.

That's how human beings are wired — or so I'd believed.

And yet, there I stood with my wooden sword raised, watching dumbly as it was batted away, not reacting until a kick to the gut sent me tumbling.

I grimaced through the pain and looked up — all four of my squadmates were on their knees. Only Krische-sama was standing.

"Something like that. Krische is strong, so it's fine. Next squad."

"Oi, Second Squad's in the way. Drag them aside."

And so, unable to recover from the pain and confusion, we were hauled to the edge of the ring.

Thanks to our sacrifice, the next squad was at least granted the chance to strike first — but their wooden swords didn't so much as graze her.

Three squads were routed in quick succession without landing a single blow, and the smirking faces of the men watching — *what are those idiots doing?* — grew grim.

Krische-sama's movements showed ample reserve. To an observer, she seemed only slightly faster than normal, and the squads that hadn't gone yet might have thought they could manage where we couldn't — yet somehow, everyone's reactions were delayed.

They stood in a daze as their swords were knocked away before they even realized she'd closed the gap, or their heads were cracked, or they were kicked sprawling. The members of this unit — the same men who had crushed the reds — looked no better than training scarecrows.

Kirik, the second-best swordsman in the unit after Kalua, was toyed with as easily as one would handle a child. The First Squad, picked from the best fighters aside from the adjutant Mia, was dispatched just as effortlessly.

In no time, forty men — without so much as clashing wooden swords, without landing a touch — were on their knees before a single person.

*Taking you all on at once would be fine.*

Only now did everyone finally understand what those words had meant.

Even if every person in this unit came at her at once, defeating them would be trivial. That was the intent behind those words.

No one was laughing anymore. The air had turned to ice.

In a real battle, all forty of us — myself included — would be dead at Krische-sama's hands. And if she felt like it, she could undoubtedly massacre everyone here.

If five couldn't even provoke a reaction, then whether it was dozens or hundreds made no difference.

Cutting down hundreds with nothing but a small curved sword meant for self-defense, a monster no seasoned warrior could match — we'd all heard the ridiculous rumors and laughed them off.

If it were some hulking strongman of inhuman stature, the stories might have been believable. But a child not even five shaku tall? No matter what, that was absurd.

Even if sycophants around the Christand young lady were exaggerating to curry favor, they'd overdone it to the point of comedy — we'd laughed.

Exaggerated though they might be, rumors were rumors. She probably wasn't *weak* — we'd thought as much. But her appearance was so far removed from our image of a warrior that we'd figured at worst, she might beat us one-on-one. Maybe.

But reality was chillingly, spine-tinglingly cold.

A child who had been munching cookies with an easy expression — the idea that this girl could effortlessly slaughter a hundred men, who could believe it?

If this had been a real battlefield, every last one of our heads would have been sent flying while we stood around oblivious.

Before Krische-sama, combat was not a contest — it was a chore.

The number or strength of opponents was nothing more than a question of how much effort it took.

With that unreasonable talent, she simply overpowered and disposed of her opponents, brooking no argument. The work was finished before they could even object.

*Beyond imagination* — this was what those words meant.

One could imagine a man like the corps commander of the Agrand Army — obviously powerful in appearance — mowing down a hundred men. But a lone girl like Krische-sama cutting down a hundred? That was beyond imagining.

If you couldn't imagine it, you couldn't understand it. If you couldn't understand it, you couldn't counter it. And if you couldn't counter it, the only outcome was to die without ever knowing why.

The Black Century — and later the Black Flag Special Service that earned its name as the world's strongest private unit — I believe the reason lies precisely there.

Whether or not you knew that someone as unimaginable as Krische-sama existed in this world.

That, I think, was the great dividing line.

Looking at us individually — myself included — none of us were exceptional. The only genius was Kalua; the rest of us were merely capable mana wielders.

Even that was more than enough compared to ordinary people, and we could have been proud of it. But Krische-sama's existence forbade us from ever growing overconfident on the battlefield.

Against enemy elites, we always attacked in groups. Never took an even fight or a favorable one, never gambled. If needed, we'd throw sand in their eyes, hurl objects, pin them down — anything and everything, no holds barred.

We crushed them with overwhelming local numbers, allowing no chance of resistance.

Whether the man before us looked strong or weak was irrelevant. We never let our guard down, and relied solely on the advantage of the group.

The battlefield was a world of unreason.

With bad luck, you might encounter Krische Christand herself. That was the battlefield.

Before unreason was forced upon you, you had to force it upon others — or die.

We were fortunate enough to be spared from "the worst kind of unreason," but afterward, we'd seen our share of monsters who wielded unreason single-handedly — Nakilus of the iron fists, Gildanstein the Black Lion, and more.

Having been confronted with the very pinnacle of unreason during training — that was what made us strong. I believe that.

Even a fourteen-year-old child could be a monster capable of killing every last one of us. If so, surely there were even more fearsome creatures out in the world — that's what we thought at the time, and with that belief, our seriousness in training only deepened.

Of course, there wasn't a second person like Krische-sama in all the world, and it was nothing more than overthinking — but if you asked us when the Black Century was truly born, it was surely that day.

By the time the forest training ended, our mindset had changed completely.

Of course, if you asked whether we'd grown fond of Krische-sama, most would have said no. But once we knew as an undeniable fact that she was a genuine prodigy, her behavior until then became at least somewhat understandable.

Not the worst kind of noble lady who looked down on commoners, but a genius girl who simply couldn't understand ordinary people — thinking of it that way took some of the edge off our anger.

That's just how prodigies are, we thought, and that resignation was at least enough to make peace with it.

Once we looked at her through that lens, Krische-sama was, in fact, overflowing with what you might call "the hallmarks of genius."

Utterly unconcerned with how others saw her, free and unrestrained. Around Selene-sama and Bery-sama, she was exactly like a child — we saw her clinging to them in public more than once.

In the best and worst sense, she was not a soldier, and not a noble.

And she was exactly the same with us.

"……Beltz. Why are we being made to practice cooking?"

"No idea. But if we've been told to, there's nothing for it."

We'd come back from the forest to the fort, expecting a second inspection. We thought she'd come to see how much we'd grown, how our attitudes had improved.

That day was field camp training. It happened to be lunchtime. While we set up tents and cooked for ourselves, Krische-sama showed up right on schedule — took one sip of the soup, and flew into a towering rage.

*'Vulture, what is this?'*

*'Ma'am, is there……some problem——'*

*'There most certainly is! There is not a shred of care, not a shred of desire to make something delicious in this soup! Is this the kind of meal that raises a soldier's morale?'*

*'No, ma'am! I wouldn't say it raises morale! Adjutant!'*

*'How many times have you had field camp exercises by now? Why haven't you tried to improve? Krische thought Vulture was a decent enough centurion, but Krische is disappointed. Setting aside hostile territory, with safety secured and time to spare, this soup is an insult to the ingredients. What on earth were you thinking to produce——'*

The captain, who had apparently earned the pet name "Vulture," was lectured before all of us.

Normally Krische-sama seemed to have a favorable opinion of the captain — she'd point things out or weigh in on policy, but never outright rejected him. This time, though, the cooking had evidently offended her so deeply that her fury was unlike anything we'd ever seen.

Dagra bowed and scraped and apologized nonstop, until finally——

*'U-um, excuse me. Today's cooking duty was our squad — First Squad and Eleventh Squad……'*

Kalua took pity, nudged Mia to speak up, and threw her to the wolves (today's cooking duty was indeed First Squad). At last Krische-sama's anger softened.

If the food had turned out badly purely because Mia was a bad cook — well, that was understandable, she could accept that. She nodded, saying *If you tried your best and it just didn't work out, that can't be helped,* but then added *Be that as it may* — and abruptly halted training for an impromptu cooking lesson.

"Mia, what is the unit's adjutant going to do if she can't even do this? It's the same as deciding which soldiers to deploy and in what order — soup has an order to its ingredients too. What are you doing, throwing everything in at once?"

"S-sorry……"

"Being clumsy aside, what matters is the intention to be careful. Bery said so too — if you earnestly think about how to make it delicious, the care comes through. Krische agrees completely. When you cook, you should properly think about the person who'll eat it——"

We listened to the cooking lesson carrying on behind us, not quite sure what to feel.

After our victory, we'd all been fired up and ready to show what we were made of — and instead we got this.

Pairs of squads made to prepare soup for ten — grown men at a training ground, wondering what on earth they were doing. The soldiers being drilled or marched past nearby kept shooting us looks that clearly said *What are these guys up to?* — and that was maddening.

We wanted to ask that question more than anyone.

I was about to toss cut ingredients into the pot when Beltz stopped me with a hand.

"Hold off on that for now. If we don't take it seriously, we'll catch hell."

"You look pretty comfortable with this. Done this before?"

"Family business originally. Got sick of it and ran away."

"Huh. Wouldn't have guessed. The knife doesn't suit you."

"Well, after that I lived pretty much like you. That part lasted longer."

Beltz grinned, then ladled some soup into a small dish and tasted it.

"Times like this, I'm actually grateful to the old man. Looking around, nobody else seems to know how to cook. Play this right and we might take first place."

His tone was confident. He'd been efficient from the start, and his knifework was impressive. Three groups had seared the meat for the soup, and his had the best color by far.

First place meant tomorrow's training was waived — nothing to do but rest, aside from coaching the last-place squad.

I had mixed feelings about all this, but if it worked out that way, it wasn't bad.

"Well, that's a relief. I'm counting on you——"

"How's this one coming? Looks not bad from here."

A voice appeared from behind — Krische-sama, materializing without a trace of presence. Despite a look that radiated nothing but presence, when she was behind you, there was simply nothing. She probably wasn't doing it on purpose, but it was murder on the heart.

"Ma'am. The soup is shaping up reasonably well."

Beltz poured some into a small dish and offered it. With an earnest expression, Krische-sama studied the dish, then closed her eyes and inhaled its scent.

She didn't taste it right away. An indescribable silence.

It was just a taste test. Shouldn't have been a big deal. And yet her expression was more serious than anything I'd ever seen from her.

The tension was contagious, and Beltz and I found ourselves exchanging glances.

After a long moment, Krische-sama slowly brought the dish to her lips. Another pause.

Like an antique dealer appraising a work of art, that unhurried deliberation — I could see Beltz swallow.

Then, slowly opening her eyes, Krische-sama glanced at the cut ingredients waiting on the board, and asked:

"Why is the corus leaf still there?"

"I-if you add it just before finishing, the, the aroma doesn't……doesn't dissipate, ma'am……"

To Beltz's slightly tense reply, the silver-haired girl furrowed her brow and nodded quietly.

"The ingredients are roughly cut, and insufficient preparation has left some astringency."

"Y-yes……my apolog——"

"However, at the same time, the careful control of heat and the thoughtful order of ingredient additions have drawn out the flavor well. That is not bad at all. Who did you make this soup for?"

"F-for the corps commander's adjutant……I thought you might prefer lighter seasoning, ma'am."

"I see. Krische had intended this to be made as a meal for soldiers exhausted from training — so perhaps Krische's instructions were partly to blame. That said, the fact that you considered who would eat it and adjusted accordingly is itself very much worth praising."

She nodded repeatedly, arms folded like some kind of master craftsman, and spoke.

"Beltz, tomorrow you will supervise cooking for Mia's squad. Vulture."

"Ma'am!"

The captain came running, snapping to attention.

"Beltz is hereby appointed provisional cooking instructor. From now on, whenever the men prepare rations, have Beltz taste them first, and have him provide guidance as needed."

"Ma'am, understood."

"Koza, you are to observe Beltz's work and learn from him. The same applies to the rest of your squad."

Having said that much, Krische-sama turned back to Beltz. Beltz hastily straightened up.

"As Krische said, this is far from a splendid meal."

"……"

"However, as field rations, it is a respectable effort. The care that went into this soup — the thought given to the person who would eat it — Krische rates that highly. Do not let this go to your head, and continue to hone your craft. Never stop your ingenuity. Krische is sure you can aim even higher."

——It was delicious.

At those added words, Beltz's eyes went wide.

"Ma'am……! Thank you, ma'am!"

He answered with a salute, looking truly happy.

Krische-sama nodded once more and turned to leave.

"Vulture, Krische is heading back."

"Ma'am! Thank you for your personal instruction."

"No need for thanks. This is Krische's job, after all……well then."

And with that, she trotted off toward the fort in her small quick steps, and every one of us watched her go.

Before the half-finished pots, nothing lingered but an indescribable, peculiar atmosphere.

"Finish the soup. We'll eat it tonight. Beltz, the corps commander's adjutant has personally nominated you — from now on, you're in charge of tasting all field rations. Teach these men the basics of cooking so we're ready in case of surprise inspections."

"Yes, sir. Understood, Captain."

"……Get back to work."

Everyone was in an indescribable mood, but the worst off was probably the captain. He'd told us over and over to prepare ourselves for Krische-sama's next inspection once we returned from the forest — and the whole thing had ended up being a cooking lesson.

He'd wanted to show her the unit's progress — the discipline, the cohesion, the loss of complacency. He'd surely been the most keyed-up of all of us — and as he walked away, an aura of sorrow trailed behind him.

He was the biggest victim, you might say. Truly pitiable.

"Hah……well, anyway, good for you, Beltz——?"

I said it, and Beltz was staring intently at the soup.

There was something nostalgic in his eyes. A faraway look.

"What's wrong?"

"No……I was remembering the old days."

"The old days……?"

He ladled some soup into a small dish and brought it to his lips.

"My old man was a hard-ass about flavor. From the very first time I cooked, he never once told me it was good. ……That's what made me hate cooking in the first place."

He closed his eyes and nodded.

"But having someone just say 'it was delicious' about a soup I made for the first time in ages……I know it's stupid, but it just made me a little happy. I guess it wasn't cooking itself I hated after all."

"I-I see……well, good for you."

Unable to say much to Beltz in his quiet, sentimental moment, I just clapped him on the shoulder.

If the birth of the world's strongest private unit was the day Krische-sama showed us her sword, then the reason Beltz and I ended up running a restaurant together was, without a doubt, that single word: *delicious*.

Showing us the sword was simply instruction. The word *delicious* was simply an honest reaction to drinking the soup. For Krische-sama, there probably wasn't any great thought behind either — she just did what came to mind, said what she felt, and that was that.

But for most other people, it's never just that.

Krische-sama's unreasonableness wasn't limited to the sword.

Her very existence was likely made of the word "unreasonable."

A single offhand action, a single word — and she'd break or derail someone's life, or change it — never noticing, never knowing, munching cookies with that easy expression.

The supreme embodiment of every natural disaster — that must be what they call Krische Christand.

I think so, from time to time.

# Chapter 334: The Dawn Crescent III

Krische-sama had several ominous epithets, but the most famous was surely "the Head-Huntress."

Later, the modifier naturally fell away and the name stood alone, but I haven't forgotten its origin.

Or rather, for us, it's more accurate to say it was unforgettable.

That battle was where we came to know Krische Christand in the truest sense.

*'The objective is to buy time until allied forces can regroup. Therefore, disruption operations against the enemy take highest priority. Limit engagements to ambushes and raids. Unless separately instructed, prepare to withdraw once the scouts have been dealt with. Do not pursue without orders.'*

Those words marked the start of a week that felt like an eternity.

The struggle for the Dragon's Maw ended in victory for Prince Gildanstein.

That said, it wasn't a total defeat, and in terms of casualty ratios, we had come out overwhelmingly ahead. I understood the logic that losing the general meant a loss was a loss, but we'd been winning every other engagement — at worst, it was a defeat by the slimmest of margins.

We'd burned the enemy alive mountain and all, and even in the retreat we'd bled them several times over. We'd rampaged through our first real battle, and as far as that part went, it was a crushing victory. For us — who'd watched Krische-sama fight right before our eyes — there was nothing left to fear, the rest of the army aside.

We were taken aback, admittedly, when Krische-sama told us a single century would hold back the entire enemy army. But we figured that with this unit and Krische-sama, it wasn't impossible.

Krische-sama — the kind of monster even the fearsome prince had fled from.

A monster among monsters who'd gone alone up the cliffside and retrieved the general's sword.

Even those who'd hated her were optimistic: if Krische-sama led us directly, any dangerous mission was manageable. We'd come out the other side as heroes, they laughed.

A young lady so crazy she'd burn an entire mountain to win. That, plus the way she reaped men's heads like mowing grass — honestly, the sheer monstrousness was starting to scare me. But as an ally, you couldn't ask for a more reassuring commander.

A monster who'd do anything to win was our monster, not the enemy's.

Despite her foster father's death, Krische-sama was calm as ever. Watching her issue orders to us in that flat, businesslike way was reassuring, and seeing her casually cut down the enemy corps commander and his adjutant in the first raid was electrifying.

We'd been tasked with eliminating enemy engineers, and the first raid was so devastating I almost felt sorry for them. A single century had taken the head of a commander leading five thousand.

On top of that, we'd killed several times our number — heroes for certain. We laughed among ourselves about how we couldn't wait to get back to the fort.

Most of us probably assumed it was over after that raid. By any standard, the results were praiseworthy for a single century.

But, as a matter of course, Krische-sama ordered the disruption operations to continue.

The situation left no choice — we grudgingly accepted that much. The first and second days still went smoothly, but in that time we'd destroyed seven centuries in total. Fatigue was starting to pile up, and men were making mistakes.

Mana wielders were superhuman compared to ordinary people, sure, but it wasn't as if we had bodies of steel — a stab would kill us just the same.

One man died, several were wounded, and finally the captain offered his recommendation to reconsider.

"……Adjutant, the men's fatigue has become impossible to ignore. With all due respect, I believe we have more than achieved this operation's objectives."

"Krische judges it insufficient. But Krische understands the situation. You all should rest for a while."

"……? Where are you——"

"As Krische said, someone will have come to inspect the fort repairs by now. Krische is going to deal with them."

She said it, then vanished into the trees. The captain stared after her, dumbfounded.

We were just as stunned, exchanging looks. Surely she wasn't planning to raid them alone.

It was hearing that exchange that first made me think something was truly wrong.

As the captain said, by any measure we'd achieved more than enough. We'd wiped out the engineers and torched the fort. Ordinary soldiers learned basic earthwork, sure, but the engineers who could actually construct a fort were carpenters and craftsmen by trade — without them, repairs would take ages.

And yet she called it insufficient? What did that mean?

The captain seemed to ponder it a while, then clapped his hands.

"Prepare for a long rest while we wait for the adjutant to return. Squads One through Ten, lie down in the center — you may sleep lightly. Everyone else, sit along the perimeter, facing outward. ……Korintz, post sentries."

"Yes, sir."

That order was probably because Krische-sama was, in the truest sense, a monster.

Having seen her fight, no one would dream of opposing her. She passed through like a wind, leaving dead men in her wake; slashed at from behind, she didn't even flinch — it was as if she had eyes in the back of her head.

Even I went through two swords, but Krische-sama used that one small curved blade. She sometimes picked up discarded swords to use, but with swords that should have been finished after ten kills, she was slaughtering over a hundred.

Slicing through the soft flesh of necks.

Even in the heat of battle, Krische-sama had the luxury of choosing where to cut. I'd initially thought she had some fixation on going for the throat, but it was simply to preserve her edge.

For Krische-sama, fighting was no different from cooking — the enemy might as well have been ingredients on a cutting board.

Her strength was beyond mortal comprehension, and that was why the captain had given up trying to stop her. A lone commander charging into the enemy was insane in the extreme, but Krische-sama was the one person who made that insanity work.

Normally you'd have to physically restrain a commander in that situation, but Krische-sama was the exception to "normally."

"Mia, isn't the bunny-girl pushing it a bit too far?"

"You think so too? I was worried about that, but……"

I caught the conversation and looked over at Kalua and the others lying nearby.

"What do you mean?"

"Before we came here, the bunny-girl was clinging to her servant and crying. The general was her foster father, so she might be pushing herself out of guilt, you know?"

"……She doesn't look like it."

"She hasn't rested properly since then, has she? After the center pulled back, she had us rest, but the bunny-girl took the rear guard herself and tore through them. And as soon as that was done, she came straight here with us."

Now that she said it, it was true. I'd seen Krische-sama take brief naps in the mountains, but while she'd ordered us to stand by, she'd gone on solo reconnaissance — and we acted on the intelligence she brought back.

We hadn't had much rest either, but Krische-sama was spending all that time running through these mountains alone, scouting. There was no way she was getting proper rest.

"The fatigue is probably showing in her movements. Did you see the sword the bunny-girl's using?"

"……? Yeah, it's amazing — with a blade like that."

"That's not what I mean. She killed all those people yesterday without a single chip in the blade. But just now, when I looked, it was chipped."

Kalua said it, arms behind her head, squinting up at the sky.

"It means she's running out of room to be careful. The bunny-girl really is absurdly strong, but with that body of hers……she doesn't look like she has exceptional stamina."

"……That's true, I suppose."

"And she's obviously childish, so……I think we should probably keep an eye on her——"

"——Kalua, stop chattering. Rest while you can — that's the job."

"Ugh. Fiiiine……understood, caaaaptain."

"Don't draw out the 'fine.'"

The captain must have noticed the talking and cut in. That was the end of it.

Nothing for it — I and the others who'd been listening closed our eyes too. Kalua's words were worth noting, but our own rest came first.

Just closing my eyes and lying flat made a world of difference for the fatigue. Even a moment of lost consciousness cleared the head considerably.

I woke from that shallow sleep to screams echoing from the mountaintop.

There was no need to guess who was causing them. The only Christand force in these mountains was us, and only one person had headed up.

The screams went on for a long time, then gradually stopped.

When Krische-sama appeared a while later, she was dripping a tremendous amount of blood from her cloak, leaving a trail.

"Vulture, water."

"Y-yes, ma'am……"

The captain quickly offered a canteen. She drank, and coughed slightly. When he reached to rub her back, she stopped him with a hand.

"Krische is fine. Continue resting. ……The enemy has retreated. They won't move right away. Tomorrow, we'll observe and decide our next move."

Then she slumped against a tree, grimacing as she removed her gauntlets and set them on the ground. Her hands were a mess — blood blisters, scrapes, covered in blood.

She pulled bandages from the small pouch at her hip, and the captain reached out.

"Allow me to tend to that. ……Bilza, medicine."

"Y-yes, sir!"

Bilza, who'd been an apothecary, came forward to treat her hands.

Krische-sama seemed to hesitate briefly, then offered a quiet "thank you" and said nothing more.

Those watching didn't say a word. They simply looked at the girl.

The next day, naturally, she led us into another raid. Half the unit was rotated out to rest, presumably out of concern for our stamina.

Only the captain accompanied her every time.

Just as Kalua had said, the limits of her endurance were plain to see. Fever seemed to be setting in — flushed cheeks, unsteady on her feet.

Yet Krische-sama still went on reconnaissance almost entirely alone, resting not at all, launching raid after raid.

Naturally the fever worsened, and each time she returned from an attack, she crumpled against a tree and slid to the ground. She ate by soaking hardtack in water, forcing it down as if even eating was too much effort, then closing her eyes and dropping into sleep like someone losing consciousness.

She was nothing like the young lady who'd watched us from the sidelines, munching cookies without a care in the world.

Even the men who'd spoken ill of her began showing concern.

This had long since ceased to be a matter of stamina.

No matter how strong she was, she was still a child.

A girl not even five shaku tall couldn't possibly outlast us. In terms of pure endurance, she'd hit her limit ages ago.

A normal person probably couldn't even stand — the exhaustion was that visible. But for mana wielders, as long as they could still manipulate mana, the body could keep moving. In the most literal sense, Krische-sama was running on willpower alone.

The captain urged retreat time and again, but she would not agree.

*'Every hour Krische and the others buy is another hour for Selene to regroup. Krische can still fight — retreating again would mean Krische could never face Selene again.'*

She'd say things like that. Other times:

*'Selene was angry at Krische for being useless. ……The head of the house died, and she cried and cried……because Krische was useless, Krische was hated by Selene……'*

She'd mumble in a haze, then drop off to sleep.

The captain told the wounded to pass along Krische-sama's condition, and soon enough a withdrawal order came bearing Selene-sama's name — but even then, she shook her head.

It was as if she were punishing herself.

*Surely by tomorrow she'll agree to withdraw* — we repeated that each day, and by the fifth, we had reached our limit too. Honestly, we wanted to flee. If our commander had been anyone else, we probably would have. We might even have staged an accident and killed her, then declared an honorable death in combat.

But Krische-sama, for better and for worse, was a child.

When a fourteen-year-old girl, covered in wounds, fought with everything she had — grown men couldn't abandon that and run, and they couldn't utter a single complaint.

Krische-sama was unfair in so many ways.

Earnest to a fault, giving it everything, not caring what anyone thought.

She would never cry out in pain, not if her arms and legs were torn off.

And on top of it all, she looked like the most lovely princess imaginable — the kind of figure that made everyone around her feel that if *she* was going to dig in, then so would they.

It was, when you came down to it, Krische-sama's selfishness.

Dragged along by it, forced into battles that could cost us our lives, and yet we couldn't complain. We put our lives on the line and followed.

In a sense, it was coerced.

Krische-sama simply made the people around her feel that way, without trying.

Whether she was feverish, whether she was at her limit — it didn't matter.

Krische-sama worked harder than anyone, killed more than anyone, saved more comrades than anyone.

Before a child like that, no one could have brought themselves to utter a word of complaint.

With each passing day, the situation worsened and Krische-sama's swordwork grew rougher.

What had been a sublime pinnacle of grace finally degraded to the point where even ordinary people like us could begin to grasp just how extraordinary it was.

Krische-sama's roughened blade was a demonic sword that chilled even those of us on her side.

"Kh……"

The small curved sword sheared through necks, or sent heads flying.

Even mass-produced blades picked up from the ground became legendary swords of fairy tales in Krische Christand's hands.

She bisected a raised sword — and the man behind it, splitting him in two at the torso — and didn't stop.

Two men cut down, and a glance to the side revealed a forest of death. Trees smeared blackish-red with the residue of lives, and things that had been human an instant ago became fountains.

How many lives had she ended in this battle?

Counting would have been absurd.

As she sprinted past, red flowers bloomed a beat behind. She seemed to wear a red wind; the crimson of lives suited her silver hair and pale cheeks well.

A thrown hand-spear turned several men into chunks of meat at once; merely running generated death.

Sometimes she snapped necks with her foot, or made them vomit their own entrails — she didn't seem to belong to the same world.

She had the form of a girl, yet she was a concept.

Not a fragment of pity, not a trace of hesitation.

She killed simply because they stood before her with swords in hand.

As a being standing on a battlefield, it was the most correct way to be.

But it was not a human way to be.

Compared to someone who could kill people for no reason other than *they were in the way* — a pleasure killer would be a far more human creature.

Krische Christand was a girl who stepped across that line with ease.

"U-uwaaahhh!!"

"Tch……!"

While my attention was stolen by her, an impact hit my body.

The soldier who'd crashed into me reeked of urine.

Knocked off balance and pushed down, the back of my helmet struck a root or something. While I was dazed, the man gripped a short sword in a reverse hold and raised it to stab me.

"Koza!"

My squadmates noticed, but they wouldn't make it in time.

*Well, I'm dead* — I stared up at the man's face with a vague sort of calm.

Half-grown kid, couldn't have been more than fifteen. Didn't even have hair on his face yet.

Before joining up here, I could have beaten a hundred of him one-on-one — an absolute amateur. This should never have been someone who could kill me, but I supposed that was how the battlefield worked, and I found it almost funny.

If there was anything pitiful about it, it was that killing me wouldn't earn him a thing. My squadmates would cut him down immediately. That would be the end of it.

At least let him get some reward for killing me, I thought vaguely, and——

"Oh——?"

Something whistled past, and the boy's head suddenly shifted. The shift kept widening — it fell from the neck, and in place of a head, a flower of blood sprouted.

I shoved the corpse off in a panic and traced the trajectory. A single longsword had embedded itself hilt-deep in a tree.

I looked toward whoever had thrown it — and there, still in the throwing stance, one hand and knee on the ground, was a head of silver hair.

Face downcast, breathing in heaves.

"Are you all right?!"

"Y-yeah……"

Looking around — the fight was all but over. I scrambled to my feet and ran to Krische-sama's side.

"You saved me! Can you stand?"

"……Krische is fine."

She answered and stood.

Drenched in an unreal amount of sweat, splattered in blood, her gaze hollow.

The monster who had sown death — and the girl who'd returned from it — looked anything but fine, no matter how you saw her.

"Vulture, withdrawal——"

She began to say it and broke into coughing. I rubbed her back, alarmed. The back I touched was astonishingly small.

Up close, the top of her head came only to the chest of a man who wasn't even particularly tall.

"Captain! Orders to withdraw!"

I shouted in her stead, and the captain, noticing, responded.

The girl — the child — heard that, drifted away from me, and broke into a run——

"Ugh——……!"

Whether her legs tangled or she nearly blacked out — she stumbled, and unable to dodge a tree, slammed her left shoulder into it hard.

She crouched on the spot, clutching her left shoulder. We rushed over, and——

"……Krische is fine."

One word, then she stood as if nothing had happened and ran.

What was fine about it, none of us could tell.

*Dead people are fine too, is that it?* — we exchanged looks and ran after that wavering figure.

Even in that state, Krische-sama told us, same as always, that disruption operations would continue. She ignored the captain's strong recommendation, and fell asleep like a puppet with its strings cut.

We could say nothing, and could only watch.

It was Kalua's words that finally secured the withdrawal, finally won permission to descend the mountain.

Yet even in the moonlight, stepping out onto the grasslands, not one man wore a smile. Everyone's attention, their concern, was on Krische-sama.

"Mia, I can take over whenever."

"It's fine. She's startlingly light."

Mia answered, holding Krische-sama bundled in a blanket.

That pained sleeping face was, by any measure, not a monster or a creature — just a child of her age.

A girl so small it seemed impossible she'd been on a battlefield.

I looked at Krische-sama's beloved sword, which had been entrusted to me, and furrowed my brow.

The beautiful blade was wrecked. That it hadn't snapped was a miracle, given the countless chips and notches. Its curve rippled like a worn-out saw. Sharp chips crushed into warped scars by flesh.

The result of a single girl taking hundreds of lives.

"Koza."

Someone called me and I handed it over. Everyone who saw it wore the same furrowed brow. Anyone with the slightest experience wielding a sword could understand the abnormality.

And it wasn't simply a matter of skill or lack thereof.

The reason she'd kept using this curved sword without letting it break was that she still intended to fight.

With a body that could barely stand, she'd kept it intact to cut down tomorrow's enemies.

If no one had stopped her by force, she would have done so until she died.

She was undeniably mad.

Kalua laughed and called her mentally ill, but I agreed with every fiber.

She never abandoned anything, and she would never allow herself to.

Even words like duty or atonement became insanity past a certain line.

But Krische-sama couldn't see that line.

The general's death was not Krische-sama's fault by anyone's reckoning, and even if she bore some small part of it, she'd more than repaid it with her performance.

Yet Krische-sama blamed herself as useless, and to fulfill a responsibility that didn't exist, she wouldn't stop even if it killed her.

The worst noblewoman became the mad genius girl.

And the day she became *our* master — if I had to name it, it was surely that walk home.

After that, I saw Krische-sama fight many more times. I witnessed feats beyond belief many more times.

But that was the first and last time I saw her fight with everything she had.

Nobody said a word about it, but perhaps it was because we all felt we should.

Let this Bernaich be the last.

——As swords of Krische Christand.

Words too embarrassing to ever actually speak aloud, and I never did — but this much I can say with certainty.

From that day forward, we all carried the same words in our hearts. Of that, I am sure.

# Chapter 335: The Dawn Crescent IV

We had done the right thing — a small mutiny born entirely of goodwill.

But put it into words, and it was seizing command and retreating without authorization.

Everyone knew we were probably in trouble.

Three days later, training resumed — and not a soul complained. Everyone threw themselves into it earnestly.

Of course, the crucible of real combat played its part. The loss of comrades, too. But more than anything, it was Krische-sama.

That battle had given us a hazy outline of who Krische-sama was as a person, but at the same time, it had eliminated any room to doubt how terrifying she was.

For Krische-sama, killing people was no more than mowing grass. Enemies were talking weeds, and she showed no mercy, no hesitation, no pity.

Something was fundamentally different in the way her mind worked.

Most of us — myself included — had our share of experience. I'd seen my share of merciless villains. But I'd never seen a human being take lives with such utter indifference.

Our actions had been goodwill, true. But whether that young lady would see it that way was highly doubtful.

Naturally, we'd also done our part in battle. We wouldn't be executed — we were fairly sure of that. But in our hearts, we'd already worked out an escape plan, "just in case."

When Krische-sama arrived about a week later——

*'Krische has various complaints, but this matter will be dropped.'*

——those words brought relief from the bottom of our hearts.

And when we were told there would even be rewards, every last man erupted in joy.

Given the scale of our achievements, we deserved at least that much — but we'd half-expected to be killed for it. Rewards instead of punishment? Of course we were in high spirits.

We got wrapped up in discussing what our unit emblem should look like — I jumped in too, arguing back and forth.

Nearly half the unit participated, everyone pitching ideas.

The density of shared time, you might call it. Going through battle together closes distances fast.

People have their preferences, and there's always one or two you don't get along with. But the kind of man you'd never share a drink with if you met him in town — that was the kind of man you could throw an arm around and laugh with, once he was a comrade-in-arms.

And the century had all kinds, for better or worse.

Farmers, men who'd never held a sword — they naturally clustered with their own kind, and bouncer types like me gravitated to our own. It was only natural when you didn't have anything to talk about.

Before the battle, the groups had been fairly segregated, but that changed completely after.

The battlefield reveals who you are. The blowhard who'd always strutted around turned out to be timid underneath, so nervous he charged in panting and tripped over his own feet. The scrawny, meek kid everyone had dismissed ran in and shielded him.

It was that harsh a battle, and every man owed his life to someone.

When the bully and the bullied threw arms over each other's shoulders and laughed, I thought — *this isn't bad at all.*

I'd had this foolish pride — the lone wolf who'd carved his way through the world on nothing but his fists. A cut above the slack-jawed faces around me, a man who didn't run with the pack. And if I died in some gutter one day, that'd be a fitting end for a life lived my way. Not a bad life, I'd thought.

But if I really believed that with all my heart, why did a man like me ever think of joining the army?

Looking back now, I think I must have been drawn, unconsciously, to that kind of circle.

The others were the same. There were plenty of men just like me.

Making friends for one night over drinks, buying love for one night with coin — both dissolving into nothing at dawn. Yesterday and today were different worlds, yet every day was the same.

Couldn't see a month ahead; thinking about the future dragged you down; numbed the ache with whatever throwaway distraction was at hand.

We all wanted something solid under our feet. That's why we'd gathered here.

Put through nonsensical training under a genius mad-rabbit commander. Cursing every single day, training to the point of death, fighting.

But then — the uproar over deciding a single unit emblem.

*What are we even getting so worked up about?* — the cool, rational part of me whispered, yet somehow, it didn't feel bad.

——How about the moon?

I don't remember who said it. Krische means the arc of the crescent moon, the person said. A well-read fellow. Probably Bilza or someone like that.

I closed my eyes and saw a moon behind my eyelids.

Freezing cold, yet undeniably lighting the world — the queen of the night.

A gentle guide, illuminating the road ahead through the dark.

A waning crescent about to disappear, and the girl from the other day overlapped with it — and behind her, the faint shapes of us, following in her wake.

*Then the banner's got to be black,* someone said. Those who march beneath the moon — the Black Century is the night that surrounds it.

*Not bad,* everyone laughed and nodded.

* * *

It was after the battle with Klare Markelus.

Kalua brought the princess to a victory celebration we'd thrown on our own initiative.

I'd always known the woman was brazen, but this was fearless beyond measure. Carried in Kalua's arms, Krische-sama looked as though she found the whole thing a nuisance. Set down on Kalua's lap, she sat silently, surveying her surroundings.

Same as always — an unreadable, expressionless face.

That changed around the time one of the soldiers asked her to peel fruit.

Drinks were flowing, decorum out the window. Still, asking the commanding officer to peel fruit — what were they thinking? And yet, strangely, Krische-sama relaxed her lips, narrowed her eyes, and wore a gentle expression.

Despite being made to peel fruit, she looked like she was having the time of her life.

Krische-sama was beautiful.

Anyone who saw her thought so. Her Majesty the Queen was her spitting image, Selene-sama was beautiful too, and everyone in the Christand household — servants included — was beautiful in their own way. But Krische-sama above all was beautiful in the air she wore, in something illusory about her presence.

They say the heart shows in the face — and it's probably true. A mean person develops mean features; a timid person, a timid face.

With Krische-sama, I think her purity was what seeped out from within.

Moonlight and blazing bonfires. Soldiers illuminated in their glow. And at their center, peeling fruit — it suited her more perfectly than anything I could imagine.

That afternoon, I'd watched her cut down dozens — including a general — in the blink of an eye, but it felt like something from a dream.

The fruit-peeling session eventually ended at Kalua's word, and from there the talk turned to the enigma herself — the young lady of the Christand household, Krische Christand.

With drink flowing and cheeks flushed, tongues loosened. Everyone was eager to hear what she'd say. Morbid curiosity, you might call it.

Less than a year since her first battle. Yet by then she'd likely killed over a thousand with her own hands, taken that many lives, and made tens of thousands of their families, lovers, and friends suffer.

Of course people were curious about a girl like that.

I was no exception.

*It's war, so it can't be helped* — there are plenty who say that. I usually think so too. But even so, there's a pang of guilt, the sort that surfaces when you're lying in bed at night.

*I put that man's life on a scale against my own — and was my life really worth taking his for?* Everyone's had a thought like that at some point, I'd wager.

Before joining the army especially, whenever my mood sank, thoughts like that would drift through my mind.

Killing a person means robbing a fellow human of their life. No matter how you dress it up, that fact can't be denied.

That's why people who kill need a cause, I think. For the country, for our comrades, for our families — we had to kill. So we could tell ourselves: *it was unavoidable.*

Maybe some can keep killing purely for their own sake, but they'd have to be exceptional fools or lunatics.

Normal people aren't like that. Sooner or later, it wears you down.

What interested me was Krische-sama's cause.

What cause had this girl used to justify taking over a thousand lives?

What logic allowed her to escape the guilt?

I'd already guessed it wasn't for her own sake. Anyone who'd been through Bernaich could see that. You can't sacrifice yourself that much for selfish reasons.

Mad she might be, but there had to be a reason — that was what I expected.

*'——Cleaning the manor, tending the garden, cooking with Bery and the others, eating meals, having tea……Krische wants to live like that every single day, forever.'*

But it was half a letdown.

Krische-sama wasn't a decent human being who fought under some banner of righteousness in the first place.

She didn't care that she'd killed a thousand. She had no interest in those she'd killed, and she didn't grant them a shred of value.

She said what she thought and acted on what she felt. When most people called her childish for that, I thought it was slightly off the mark.

Compared to us, her way of perceiving things was simply far more basic — simple enough, extreme enough, to kill a thousand without hesitation for the sake of a peaceful, happy daily life.

Honestly, it sent a chill through me.

A modest wish, certainly. Most probably found it heartbreaking and pitiable.

But flip it around, and a girl who could kill people without flinching for the sake of such a trivial happiness — that was Krische Christand. A madwoman among madwomen, nothing more.

Everything outside what concerned her own happiness was probably irrelevant to her. And anything that threatened her happiness was unforgivable.

That logic didn't exempt herself — Bernaich had been the result.

One baseless conviction — *I was useless, Selene hates me* — had driven her to the brink of death and left hundreds dead. That wasn't sane.

Some would be angry to hear Krische-sama called a madwoman, but my view hasn't changed since then. Without question, Krische-sama was abnormal, a person who belonged on that side of the line.

Someone who could render everything outside her beloved "modest daily life" worthless — if that wasn't a madwoman, no one could answer what was.

Watching Krische-sama, I sometimes thought: *love, at its core, is discrimination.*

Wanting to be good to someone, wanting to make someone happy — no one would fault those feelings. But the world is complicated. Making one person happy can mean making another miserable.

A decent person weighs the scales in their mind each time, measuring what matters more. But Krische-sama probably didn't agonize over such things.

For her, everything was clear-cut: one or zero.

Simple, extreme, without hesitation.

I've never known a person as full of love as Krische-sama.

I've never known anyone as sincere, as earnest, as pure.

And I feel it, honestly — there's something genuinely beautiful about the way she is.

But decent folk are messier things.

They fret over how others see them, scramble not to be mocked, get tangled up in obligations, flail and struggle and live pathetic, undignified lives.

And yet, they fight to cover their own ugliness and live as best they can — that's what being human is.

A princess who could remain beautiful without ever knowing, let alone respecting, that struggle.

If someone told me *this* is the right way to be, then what were the rest of us?

That was exactly why Krische-sama was a madwoman.

"——Actually, you know, Bery can even talk to cats."

"Cats?"

"Yes. Krische was watering the orchard one day, and a cat wandered into the garden, and Bery just went up to it and said 'meow,' and started chatting. And then she translated the cat's words for Krische, and taught Krische how to talk to them too. So every time Krische sees a cat, Krische tries a little bit, but——"

No climax, no punchline.

All Krische-sama talked about was inane nonsense from the manor.

Even with drinks flowing, not a single laugh.

She had no thought for how her audience felt. She rattled off every "fun story" she could think of, one-sidedly, and never was there a worse-tasting drink.

But we — myself included, the rank and file — could only sit there and listen without complaint.

They say names reveal nature, and with Krische-sama, it was true.

A moon that illuminates everything equally from a place no one can reach.

If she'd stayed up in the clouds, we could have accepted it for what it was. But she insisted she was human like us, then proceeded to discriminate without batting an eye — the height of unreason.

The bonfire drowned out even the stars. Only the moon hung alone in the sky.

Around it, the sky turned indigo, repainting the dark just a little.

Without it there, the view would have been awfully lonely.

So one madwoman — that was fine.

At dawn, the moon would naturally fade and melt into the sky.

"Beltz, let's get up a little early tomorrow."

"What a coincidence. I was just thinking the same."

"Had some truly awful booze tonight. Need a palate cleanser."

"That we do."

By the time we'd been discussing the unit banner, my mind was already made up. That night was just confirmation.

To learn the reason for marching under the moon, until the dawn.

# Chapter 336: The Dawn Crescent V

The end of the civil war was a hard fight for us.

We knew Krische-sama's strength. We knew our own weakness. We'd survived the madness of Bernaich, and our solidarity was the strongest it had ever been.

And still, many died.

Before being reorganized into the Black Flag Special Service, on that day, we were without doubt the finest century there ever was.

Even accounting for the captain's absence, morale was at its peak. Entrusted with the princess Selene-sama, we were ready to fight with our lives on the line — literally.

Not long ago, we hadn't known the first thing about coordination, but since then, we'd fought on the front lines through three campaigns — Markelus, Hilkintos, Wirring.

On that day, we had the finest unity and courage imaginable — I'll swear to that no matter what anyone says.

But the reason monsters are called monsters is that they can overwhelm even a force like that through sheer power alone.

Through every battle until then, we'd surrounded and killed plenty of mana wielders. Among them, sure, there were men I could never have beaten one-on-one.

But if one man wasn't enough, two would do — or three, or four. Strike without mercy. Stab them in the back. That was the Black Century.

If needed, throw sand, hurl a weapon, pin them down — anything to kill.

Only the strong can afford to call something cowardly.

We knew in our bones that we were the weak ones on the battlefield, the ordinary men. Dirty, crude — but survive and a win is a win. The dead don't get to complain.

We'd refined that way of fighting, and the coordination was so smooth it didn't even require a signal.

No matter how fearsome the warrior, as a group we could always win — that was our shared conviction.

But it was just one man.

We were ready to lay down our lives. We fought dirty. We used our numbers.

And still, we couldn't kill that one man.

The giant who swung great axes in both hands — his name was Nakilus Ferizar.

Every man who stood before him had his guts scattered.

Every single one of them was a man among men. Not a hero from a fairy tale could have matched them.

The most magnificent bastards in the world.

Not a one of them had to be asked — they just stepped up, in the heat of the moment, and threw away their one and only life for their comrades.

Some said they'd go home and tell their kids *Papa's a hero.* Some said they'd propose to the girl at that shop. Some just wanted to drink until they drowned. Paltry, laughable dreams.

But to the man who held each one, it was worth more than any great cause.

And every last one of them — the monster scattered.

The world is not equal. Bundled dreams, all that brilliance — shattered by a single being's single will.

And even that monster, Krische-sama trampled without a thought.

I was relieved, but I couldn't help wondering: *what are the rest of us?*

If Krische-sama hadn't come, Mia would have died; we all might have been wiped out. I understood the logic — we'd bought time, we'd done our job.

But feelings aren't logic.

After the battle, gathering the remains of our comrades — that feeling is something only those who've been through it can understand.

The nauseating guts and severed limbs — strangely, they didn't make me sick.

*If you're gonna die, die cleaner, you bastard* — we'd grumble now and then, laughing.

*'Oy……that's my hand you've got there.'*

Even half-dead Daz was wearing the same face, laughing.

There was nothing to do but laugh.

And that night, we drank.

Not maudlin — drinking hard, raising hell, laughing.

We were never anything special to begin with. Men like me, living day to day. Failed craftsmen. Dirt farmers. Sheep herders.

Everywhere you looked, a dime a dozen — and picking up a few tricks didn't change that.

Accept it and you're back to normal.

The world is moved by a tiny handful of the few, and we weren't them. We knew that. We'd always known.

Fragile lives, no more than insects in the grand scheme.

Living in the moment, drinking, eating, raising hell — if we could laugh through it, that was enough.

And so we welcomed Krische-sama when she appeared at the feast.

We were insects, maybe, but Krische-sama wasn't.

She'd cleaved the monster Ferizar clean in two. Taken the prince's head in a single stroke when even Kalua and the others couldn't touch him.

In the paintings of this princess, we'd be somewhere in the background.

Soldiers who followed the peerless great hero. For the likes of us insects, that was more than enough — change to spare.

A victory scene should be a lively one, so the noise and revelry fit right in. We showered praise on Krische-sama's unhinged feats.

Stick with you, and we've got nothing to fear — that's what.

Sadly, though, our princess had no shortage of flaws.

*'Krische only helped out a little at the end. In that place, in that situation — even without Krische, you had the advantage, and that was thanks to this unit's performance. You all worked hard to fulfill Krische's order to protect Selene.'*

Our tone-deaf princess came out with words like that.

*'It is a shame Krische cannot say it to those who died. They disobeyed Krische's orders by dying, so Krische wanted to praise them and scold them both. ……The fools.'*

Though she'd killed people like insects, she looked down with those words.

Not laughing happily — probably picturing their absent faces, the crescent missing from the whole.

She doused cold water on our celebration, was carried off by Kalua, and left.

The feast went on after that, but one by one, men drifted away.

Enough disappeared and the feast wound down.

I went back to my cot in the tent and closed my eyes. What I heard was pathetic sobbing.

Grown men, shameful. *Somebody throw out whoever's bawling,* I thought, but instead I just turned my back on the sound.

The princess was a mass of unreason who trampled over people without a thought.

She probably never considered how those she killed had felt.

Compared to her, the monster Ferizar was more human by far.

He'd had conviction, at least. And the fight between him and us — us, the nameless insects — had been a clash of those convictions.

Even as enemies, I could respect the man for what he was. He'd been something else.

Honor, conviction, pride — we'd both fought carrying something like that, so you could accept it. *It couldn't be helped,* you could tell yourself.

We'd both fought desperately for something we couldn't bend. It wasn't just killing — it was a noble, exalted exchange of lives.

Not one of the men who died that day had any regrets.

There couldn't have been a finer place to die.

And yet, knowing nothing of what the lowly felt or why they fought, caring nothing——

That princess kicked aside stones on the road and said it.

——*It's a shame they died.*

The most obvious thing in the world, spoken without reading the room. Casually, without a thought for why no one else had said it.

As if hearing that a neighbor had met with an accident. *They were so full of life just yesterday* — that was the level of it.

Our princess, Krische Christand, did not understand the human heart.

* * *

The Black Century became the Black Flag Special Service.

Aside from nearly being stuck in a baffling outfit called the "Kuro-Fuyo Squad," there isn't much more to tell from there.

A maiden's unwanted heroic epic, carrying on until dawn.

We just ran with the moon held high.

Of course, if I sat and remembered every little thing, there'd be no end. No shortage of funny stories and stupid stories — but the truly hard times were that one stretch and no more.

Krische-sama was unreasonable and utterly without charm, and never made the same mistake twice. That era was, in a word, a stage built for Krische-sama.

The other side must have found it intolerable.

Every enemy who stood in the way was a villain destined to fall, and thanks to some cheap, absurd script, even the fiercest warrior was cut down in one stroke and sent offstage.

Krische-sama was stronger than anyone, cleverer than anyone — that kind of invincible hero.

On top of it all, as a protagonist she was third-rate, with zero interest in entertaining the audience. She crushed everything in her path, then hurried backstage to happily do behind-the-scenes work.

If I were in the audience, I'd have a few words to say — but I was cast as a soldier in her retinue. My job was to attend to the whims of a leading lady who'd rather live in the wings.

*If only this wretched show would end already,* I thought every single day.

Then the star could live backstage forever and nobody would complain. She'd never have to take the stage she didn't want, and she'd never be called to the lead role again.

Krische-sama had not one shred of interest in rank, fame, or power.

The King's Elder Sister and Alberinea. She could have sat idly and wanted for nothing — all the world would have been given to her if she'd asked — yet she preferred to play at being a servant in an apron dress.

Cold and ruthless, viewing people as less than human — and yet she'd say *working for the sake of others is a wonderful thing.* The contradiction was breathtaking.

Or perhaps, precisely because she couldn't see them as human, she wished she could.

She was called a cursed child of the royal line, and truly, Krische-sama was a madwoman.

She couldn't have killed people that mercilessly otherwise.

Krische-sama was broken at the most fundamental level — and she hated that about herself. Enough years together, and even I understood that much.

In her heart of hearts, social values probably meant nothing to her.

War or peace, a child being cut down before her eyes, entrails spilled on the ground — she could stand there with a cookie in her mouth and not bat an eye. That was who Krische-sama was.

She felt no pity, no sorrow — couldn't feel it. She simply registered what she saw — *cut down, entrails out* — and felt nothing beyond that.

And yet, once a relationship was formed, she became startlingly good-natured.

She'd find reasons to craft prosthetic limbs for her unit members. No matter what rudeness Kalua said or did, she'd at most throw a childish tantrum.

The magic weapons, too — if you traced them to their origin, they were built so that *her people* wouldn't die anymore. That was more or less the reason behind them.

With people who weren't under her command, it was even more pronounced. No matter what was done to her, she never forgot her courtesies, bowing and scraping to everyone.

She could be so good-natured it made you nervous — but only within her circle. The moment something fell outside it, she could render it all worthless.

The main reason Krische-sama aspired to be a servant, I sometimes thought, was simply so she wouldn't have to show that side of herself.

A small, enclosed world where everything was *inside* and nothing lay beyond. In a place like that, Krische-sama could remain good-natured, and no one would call her mad.

Even unifying the world — that monumental achievement — probably had just that one reason behind it.

Not a princess who loved peace, but a deranged girl who needed peace just to hide the madness. And so she annihilated every enemy that stood in the way. That was all.

Some would deny it, but for me, this explanation makes the most sense.

*'——Bery said so too.'*

That was Krische-sama's signature phrase, the one everyone knew. The name that came up at every turn — Bery-sama — was her servant.

True to the Christand tradition, she was a strikingly beautiful woman, but I hadn't seen her all that often. She visited Krische-sama from time to time, but Krische-sama never once brought her before us. Her Majesty the Queen was more familiar to us, if anything.

When she did bring a servant along, it was always Kalua's younger sister, Elvena — never Bery-sama.

Grandfather Gallen-sama, former Marshal's Aide Faren, and the servant Bery-sama.

In all the world, only three people earned Krische-sama's unreserved praise — and Bery-sama especially. Whenever Bery-sama happened to appear, those two were in their own world.

Everything — conversations, training, work — was dropped the instant she arrived. And at the manor, too, it was the same.

But she never brought that woman along. Why?

The answer that fit best, when I thought about it, was something embarrassingly ordinary: she didn't want to show her worse side to the woman she loved.

A perfectly normal motive anyone could understand.

Normally you'd try to change yourself so that side never showed — but changing the world instead was simply the Krische-sama brand of unreasonable.

Rather than a pure and innocent child who loved peace, she was a hopelessly troublesome maiden in love.

To me, that's who Krische-sama was — and a hopeless madwoman all the same.

* * *

——I opened my eyes, and the morning sun was blinding.

Sitting up felt like a chore. I laughed — *haven't kicked the bucket yet, have I?*

Looking out the window, there was that preposterous tree in full bloom, its flowers shimmering in rainbow hues.

Utterly inconsiderate of anyone around it — the sheer scale was Krische-sama all over.

I wondered how many people could understand, if told this was built as a love nest to hide away in. But anyone who *could* understand was as mad as she was.

The boys had come in a panic — was it last night?

In the sky above, a faint crescent moon was visible.

My body felt remarkably well.

Might as well show my face at the restaurant, I thought, and stood.

The old man in the mirror looked like he could drop dead at any moment. Bald on the forehead and crown — I was in no position to laugh at the captain.

Though the captain had just shaved his head. He hadn't actually been bald. Probably.

Scratching at my unkempt beard, I splashed water from the basin on my face and stepped into the hall.

An overly grand, long corridor. Thanks to generous fellows who said they'd pay in booze, we'd ended up with a handsome three-story inn, but in old age, it wasn't the most convenient.

Getting older meant being shunted to the far end of the second floor, which made the corridor feel even longer.

Lively voices drifted up from the first floor. Today's crowd was a good one, by the sound of it.

"Pops, are you sure about this? Up and walking all of a sudden?"

Coming up the stairs was an old woman, her tone exasperated.

The daughter who'd once had skin like porcelain was now a wrinkled, nagging old crone.

*We've gotten old,* I laughed, and leaned on her shoulder.

"Stairs are too much trouble. I want to eat downstairs for once. Take me."

"……Honestly. And if you fall on your own?"

"Fine way for me to die, wouldn't you say."

She sighed. I laughed and let her bring me down.

The spacious dining floor was full of patrons, all buzzing about that tree.

At the counter, a few old men from the neighborhood sat drinking from the morning.

"What, Tagel, you're still alive too?"

"Look who's talking. I heard you were bedridden."

"Probably dead by tomorrow."

*Here,* I said to my daughter, and took the seat next to Tagel.

The honored sergeant of the Black Century. He'd always been a handsome man, and even on death's doorstep as an old codger, that hadn't changed. The neatly trimmed beard was so like him.

"I felt the same way, but today I woke up in great shape."

"What a coincidence. For the first time in a while, even the mana's cooperating."

"That tree's probably the reason……but either way, it won't last long. While I'm still feeling good, I thought I'd have one last meal here."

Tagel soaked bread in the lamb stew and popped it into his mouth. The scent that tickled my nose wasn't half bad.

A bit heavy for a morning soup, but the herbs gave it a fresh finish.

*Made it the way I taught them,* I thought with satisfaction, nodding.

"Kuuri, same for me."

"You got it."

My daughter set a glass of water in front of me and headed for the kitchen.

"I thought I wouldn't wake up this morning. But I spent last night remembering old times, and it wasn't a bad feeling."

"Think of it as Krische-sama's gracious consideration — letting you see your own shop one last time before you die. We've lived long enough, after all."

"Optimistic bastard. Can't stand it."

"I intend to enjoy every last moment. Once I've painted that tree, I can die happy."

Listening to him, I tilted the glass and drank. Behind me, idiotic laughter — what was there to be so happy about this early in the morning?

Drinking from the morning — a pack of good-for-nothings.

I took Tagel's wine bottle and poured some into my emptied glass.

"You'll catch hell for that."

"You're sneaking drinks behind your family's back too. At least I'm doing it out in the open."

"Oh, by this evening, someone from here will be bringing bread over and will be sure to tell one of mine. I'm drinking in full expectation of the lecture."

I eyed the smiling Tagel and brought the wine to my lips.

Cheap wine, but not bad. When it's been long enough, anything tastes fine.

"Well, no quarreling. I want to enjoy my last drink in peace."

"Agreed. For the first time in a while, I'm in the mood for some old stories."

"When you're this old, what else is there to talk about?"

I held out my glass toward the bottle in Tagel's hand.

"……The moon's faded away somewhere. A fine time for that kind of talk."

# Chapter 337: The Dawn Crescent VI

The Unification War had ended, and some time had passed since the return to the capital.

They had been gathered in the reception room of an old estate in the capital's castle district.

Everyone present was a member of the Black Flag Special Service who held some kind of rank.

"A covert unit?"

"Krische-sama wants to use Black Flag personnel to establish a unit focused primarily on intelligence gathering and covert operations. The name is……"

For the initial startup, we're hoping to have the captain cooperate in an advisory role for a while."

When Mia said this, the captain smiled and nodded.

The captain had seemed to age all at once since the Unification War ended.

"Consider it my final duty as military personnel. I'd love to see it through to the end, but……"

"That's more than enough. Fufu, Krische-sama told me not to push you too hard, after all."

We laughed at Mia's words.

Throughout the Unification War, Krische-sama had shown considerable concern for the captain.

*'Vulture, make sure you eat properly and go to bed early. No staying up late. Mia, if Vulture's health declines, it'll be your fault.'*

*'Ehh……?'*

*'See to it accordingly.'*

How many times we'd heard exchanges like that, no one could count.

"……That said, well, I think Krische-sama has all sorts of plans in her head, but we haven't been properly briefed on everything yet. For now, it seems like she wants to recruit volunteers."

"……? Volunteers?"

"Yeah. The continent is vast, and of course the more people we have the better, but……the war's over. Krische-sama said not to force anyone."

Mia said this happily.

"'The Kuro-Fuyo worked very hard and more than earned back their investment cost, so Krische doesn't want to ask too much of them'——that's what she said."

At those words, I felt the tension leave my shoulders, as if by some strange magic.

How very like Krische-sama.

"Next month, when the Black Flag Special Service officially disbands, Krische-sama plans to make the announcement. But before telling everyone, she wanted to get the general framework sorted out to some extent, so today we gathered people like you, Koza, who could potentially be entrusted with command. If you want to join, we'd of course be happy, but Koza and Beltz, you two were talking about opening a shop, weren't you?"

"Well, yeah, but……"

Beltz and I exchanged glances.

Truth be told, just the other day we'd gone together to scout out a location.

A small inn that served meals.

Originally, I'd wanted to run a place that just served food three times a day, but in the capital, the rules restricted how land could be used depending on the type of business.

In short: don't waste land, keep it to the bare minimum.

Running a regular shop or restaurant was no problem, but what Beltz and I had in mind wasn't some cozy little place — it was a space large enough for a hundred or more former squad members to gather.

Since the application for a mere restaurant wouldn't get approved, we'd decided to set it up as an inn.

The capital was lenient when it came to inns.

According to someone who knew the details, the castle district in particular encouraged them to promote the flow of people, and the taxes were apparently cheaper than for apartment buildings.

It aligned with our goal of running a place that served food, and being an inn wasn't a bad idea either.

We'd been going around lightly introducing ourselves to the wholesale food shops and doing various preparatory things.

Nothing had been finalized yet, and we hadn't signed any contracts.

A slight delay wouldn't have been a problem, but it was honestly a tough call.

"By the way, would this work go on forever?"

When Beltz asked, Mia shook her head.

"No. She says in about ten years, we'll be out of a job. You know the Zenith, right?"

"Hm? Oh, yeah……"

"Apparently that thing will blanket this planet with mana in roughly that amount of time. Once it does, Krische-sama and Her Majesty the Queen will be able to handle everything themselves."

"……She's really starting to sound like an actual god."

I said, exasperated.

Anyone who'd been at the Battle of Vezrea could understand that there was nothing Krische-sama couldn't do if she set her mind to it.

When she said she could handle everything, she probably really could handle everything.

"Tell me about it. Well, anyway, that's the situation, so anyone who isn't ready to commit on the spot, take some time to think it over. Krische-sama will come by next week or so, so just let her know your decision then."

* * *

It was a tavern nearby.

At a corner table, it was just the three of us — me, Beltz, and Tagel — sipping wine and nibbling on sausages and cheese.

"Sorry, but……I won't be able to join."

"……Tagel."

"I made my wife and kids lonely and worried for too long. I promised them this was the last time — that once I retired, I'd take over my father-in-law's bakery and become a baker. ……If there's a rebellion and they actually need my sword, then of course I'll take it up for Krische-sama's sake, but……this isn't that kind of thing."

Tagel said, stabbing a sausage with his fork.

"And unlike the battlefield, this kind of work inevitably makes you resented. If by some chance something happened to my family because of it……I just can't join."

"Well, can't be helped. Don't beat yourself up over it."

"Yeah. We need you baking bread for our shop anyway."

When I said that, Tagel nodded and put the sausage in his mouth.

He washed it down with wine, then asked.

"What about you two? You'd been doing all that preparation, hadn't you?"

"That's the thing. A few years might be fine, but ten years is a lot……Koza, what about you?"

"I thought about it a bit, and……I figure, why not just run the shop on the side?"

"……?"

I said, wetting my tongue with wine.

"The capital's big enough. There's no rule saying there has to be only one base, right? That place is the official first base, and our shop is the second base. We won't be like the guys running around outside, but picking up intel from the gripes of drunks wouldn't be half bad either. ……Krische-sama's the kind of person who doesn't understand people's hearts, so she probably wouldn't think of something like that."

"……I see."

"More like cooperators than members of that Kuro-Shutan or whatever. Running the shop while lending them a place, picking up on rumors and the like……if it's that kind of arrangement, I figure we could be of some use."

I looked at Tagel, as if to say I understood his concerns.

"I doubt there's anyone who'd mess with a former squad member's family right under Krische-sama's nose. We're full-fledged Senior Knights, one-generation nobles or not — we're genuine nobility. Nobody's going to pick a fight unless they've got a death wish. Any incident would blow up — the risk is way too high."

"I said 'by some chance,' didn't I. I just don't want to make unnecessary enemies, as a peace-loving citizen of the kingdom. Unlike you, I've lived a life of integrity."

"Hah, sorry for being raised in the back alleys."

"Knock it off, you two."

Beltz said with an exasperated look, then nodded.

"What you just said, though — that's not bad."

"Right? Once the work over there wraps up, we can just focus on the shop. It doesn't sit right with me to do whatever we please without helping them out at all."

"That's what was weighing on me too……as a compromise, it's first-rate. Shall we bring it to Krische-sama?"

"Yeah——"

And so the following week, Krische-sama appeared before us and explained everything.

Since we'd already heard most of it from Mia, no one's face showed confusion.

After listening to Krische-sama's explanation, Beltz and I stepped forward.

"——So that's what we've been thinking."

We'd also consulted with Mia and the others, and had worked out the finer details to some degree.

I'd assumed we'd get an immediate yes, but Krische-sama, sitting on the sofa with a cup of tea in hand, wore a troubled expression.

Beltz and I exchanged looks at her lukewarm reaction.

"Um……Krische-sama, is there a problem?"

"No, it's not that there's a problem……the two of you want to run a shop that serves delicious food, right?"

"Yes, well……"

"Then Krische thinks it would be better for you to devote yourselves fully to that."

She made an entirely valid point.

Had the concept of a compromise failed to get across? Just as I was feeling exasperated, Krische-sama gazed at the cookies on the table and spoke with a nostalgic air.

"It was a long, long time ago. Krische was talking about what-ifs with Bery."

"……What-ifs?"

"Yes. Wouldn't it be nice if the Christand house were a merchant family instead, Krische said. ……Then Krische and the others would be at peace, the war wouldn't concern us at all. It would probably be so much fun every day."

She picked up one honey-colored cookie and put it in her mouth.

Smiling happily.

"Bery told Krische that if she was going to imagine things, she should imagine what-ifs about the future instead……and sometimes the two of them would talk about those kinds of dreams. ……For example, Bery said she'd like to run a restaurant someday — the same kind of thing Koza and the others are planning."

Then she quietly closed her eyes.

"We'd go incognito, come up with all sorts of dishes, and have all sorts of customers enjoy delicious food. Krische and Bery would do the cooking, and we'd make Kreschenta and Selene help despite their complaints, and have Anne and the others serve the tables……when Krische imagined it, it sounded so, so fun."

She placed her hand on her chest — on the mana crystal necklace hidden beneath her clothing.

Bery-sama had passed away long ago, but Krische-sama had brought her soul back — that's what we'd heard from Kalua.

Or rather, it was probably impossible to hide from anyone who'd been worried about Krische-sama.

When Krische-sama appeared before us after returning from the battle with Vezrea, she was strangely, remarkably well.

Bery-sama still slumbered within that mana crystal hanging from her neck.

She was surely picturing that sight behind her closed eyelids.

When she opened her eyes, those purple irises turned toward us.

Like jewels — that was the common comparison for those inorganic eyes, but strangely, I no longer felt the coldness they once held.

"Krische thinks it's a wonderful dream — wanting to run a shop that serves delicious food, like Koza and Beltz do. But if you're going to do it, Krische wants you to give it your all — not as a side project, but thinking only of making the people who eat there happy."

"……Krische-sama."

"The Kuro-Shutan will be fine as long as there are enough people. You don't need to worry about it."

Krische-sama said, and smiled.

"If it's you two, who always put your hearts into cooking, Krische is sure you'll make a wonderful shop……ehehe, Krische will cheer you on with all her heart."

And then she said something like, "When it's open, Krische will come eat there," leaving us absolutely no room to argue.

- How to read kingdom-noble names

Example: 1, Gildanstein = 2, Kalnaros = 3, Vel = 4, Sarcarinea = 5, Alberan

1, Given name: Gildanstein

2, The largest territory among the lands they administer: Kalnaros.

Properly the entire administered region is included, making it very long, and so this is abbreviated.

3, Vel for male royals with right of succession; Vera for female royals.

4, Honorary title: Sarcarinea

5, Surname: Alberan

Properly between 4 and 5 a military rank such as Marshal or General, or a court office such as Chancellor, would be inserted, and after the surname a peerage rank such as Grand Duke would follow.

But the foreign-letter strings are already long and the eye slides off, so within the text we attach the necessary rank or office at the end and write it in kanji for clarity.

And in the imperial tournament to which he invited her, witnessing a transcendent technique beyond his imagining, seeing the true moon, he felt shame at the breadth of the world; afterwards he renamed himself Tetsushūsai, and added *Koten* (Lone Sky) to his school's name.

Never reachable, high in the heavens, but assuredly existent — that sword was no illusion.

The form of beauty he had once sought from the bottom of his heart, regaining a youth-like vigor.
